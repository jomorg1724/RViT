# Receptive fields and functional architecture of monkey striate cortex

## 1. Abstract

Hubel & Wiesel recorded extracellularly from single neurons in the striate cortex (area 17, V1) of lightly anaesthetised macaque and spider monkeys while presenting moving and stationary spots, slits, dark bars, and edges of varying orientation, position, direction of motion, and binocular configuration. The paper extends to primate cortex the simple/complex/hypercomplex receptive-field taxonomy that the authors had previously established in cat (Hubel & Wiesel 1962, 1965), and reports three principal results that go beyond the cat. First, the **basic receptive-field categories transfer to monkey**: simple cells with spatially-segregated antagonistic subregions, complex cells with position-invariant orientation tuning, and hypercomplex (end-stopped) cells are all present and qualitatively indistinguishable from their cat counterparts; however, macaque receptive fields are substantially **smaller** than cat fields, and orientation tuning is correspondingly **sharper**, matching the higher visual acuity of the primate fovea. Second, the **columnar architecture is richer in primate**: orientation columns (cells of similar preferred orientation arranged vertically through the cortical depth) coexist with **ocular-dominance columns** (alternating bands of left-eye and right-eye dominance), and the two systems are *independent* — orientation preference varies smoothly across the surface within each ocular-dominance band, yielding a two-dimensional joint map of orientation and eye-of-origin overlaid on the retinotopic surface map. Third, the **laminar organisation of receptive-field type is systematic**: layer IV (especially IVA and IVB) is dominated by simple cells and by monocularly-driven cells reflecting the unmixed geniculate input; layers II, III, V, and VI contain predominantly complex and hypercomplex cells, most of which are binocularly driven, reflecting the intracortical processing that builds primate V1's higher-order receptive-field properties. A small fraction of cells exhibit colour-coding (later elaborated as the "blob" / cytochrome-oxidase-rich compartments). The paper establishes the primate V1 functional architecture that is the empirical anchor of every subsequent model of human and machine vision: simple → complex → hypercomplex hierarchy, oriented Gabor-like receptive fields, retinotopic organisation, orientation columns, and ocular-dominance columns are all confirmed and quantified in the species directly relevant to human and primate-derived AI models.

The authors emphasise that the *qualitative* generalisation from cat to primate is near-complete — the same receptive-field categories, the same column logic, the same retinotopic surface map — while the *quantitative* details differ in the direction expected from primate visual capabilities: smaller receptive fields near the fovea, sharper orientation tuning, an additional and clearly-resolved ocular-dominance column system, and a more strongly stratified laminar specialisation in which the geniculate-recipient layer IV is the dominant locus of simple and monocular cells. The paper thereby provides the empirical bridge from the cat work to the species that supports almost all subsequent primate-centric systems neuroscience and to which human visual cortex is closely homologous.

## 2. Why this matters for us

## 3. Key claims

1. **Simple, complex, and hypercomplex receptive-field classes generalise from cat to macaque**: all three classes are present in monkey V1 with qualitatively identical properties, establishing the cat→primate generality of the 1962/1965 taxonomy.
2. **Macaque V1 receptive fields are smaller than cat fields**, particularly near the fovea, with correspondingly **sharper orientation tuning**, matching the higher visual acuity of the primate fovea.
3. **Orientation columns exist in macaque V1**: cells of similar preferred orientation cluster vertically through cortical depth, with orientation preference varying smoothly across the surface in steps of roughly 10–20° between adjacent columns.
4. **Ocular-dominance columns are a primate specialisation**: alternating bands of left-eye- and right-eye-dominated cells run across the cortical surface, with band widths of roughly 0.25–0.5 mm. Layer IV is most strongly segregated; outer layers show progressively weaker eye-of-origin specificity due to intracortical mixing.
5. **The orientation and ocular-dominance column systems are independent**: orientation preference varies smoothly across both eye-dominance bands, so each surface location encodes both an orientation and an eye-of-origin, with the two dimensions arranged orthogonally over the cortical sheet.
6. **Laminar specialisation of cell type**: layer IV (especially IVA and IVB) is dominated by simple cells, most of which are monocularly driven, reflecting unmixed input from the lateral geniculate nucleus; layers II, III, V, and VI contain predominantly complex and hypercomplex cells, most of which are binocularly driven.
7. **The functional architecture builds the primate visual computation in stages**: geniculate concentric-centre-surround inputs converge to simple cells in layer IV, which converge to complex cells in supragranular and infragranular layers, which converge to hypercomplex cells — the simple → complex → hypercomplex hierarchy is laid down anatomically across cortical layers.
8. **A small subpopulation exhibits colour coding**, presaging the blob / cytochrome-oxidase-rich compartments that Livingstone & Hubel later mapped in detail.
9. **Direction-of-motion selectivity** is present, especially among complex cells, and shows regional variation across the cortical surface, indicating that motion-direction is yet another feature dimension that V1 represents over the retinotopic sheet.
10. **Retinotopy is preserved** as in cat, with central visual field disproportionately represented on the cortical surface (cortical magnification factor), so that the joint map carries retinotopic position, orientation, ocular dominance, and motion direction as orthogonal feature dimensions over the V1 sheet.
11. **The functional architecture is the same across the two monkey species sampled** (macaque and spider monkey), establishing that the column / lamina structure is a general primate feature rather than a macaque idiosyncrasy.
12. **Orientation-non-specific units** appear preferentially in the geniculate-recipient sublayers of layer IV; these are interpreted as either geniculate afferent terminals or stellate cells receiving them directly, and provide the unoriented concentric-centre-surround substrate from which the orientation-tuned simple cells in adjacent sublayers are built.

## 4. Methods

**Preparation.** Adult macaque and spider monkeys were lightly anaesthetised and paralysed; the eyes were held open, refracted, and dilated for the recording geometry. Visual stimuli — spots, slits, bars, and edges of variable orientation, position, direction of motion, and contrast polarity — were projected on a screen at controlled angles and velocities, with independent stimulation of either eye or both eyes simultaneously to assess binocular interaction and ocular dominance.

**Recording.** Extracellular single-unit recordings were obtained from striate cortex using tungsten microelectrodes inserted at angles ranging from approximately normal to the cortical surface (radial penetrations probing through the laminar depth) to tangential (penetrations across the surface at fixed depth). The penetrations sampled all cortical layers and crossed multiple orientation columns and ocular-dominance bands. Electrolytic lesions were placed at the end of representative penetrations and the brains processed histologically (Nissl staining of cortical sections), so that the precise cortical depth and surface position of every recorded cell could be reconstructed and assigned to a specific cortical lamina and to a specific position relative to the ocular-dominance and orientation maps.

**Receptive-field mapping.** Each isolated cell's receptive field was plotted by hand by moving a stationary spot of light or dark across the screen and noting regions of excitation and inhibition. Once subregion structure (if any) was identified, the cell was probed with oriented bars, slits, and edges at the preferred position over the full 180° of orientations to characterise orientation tuning. The cell was classified as simple if the receptive field had discrete excitatory and inhibitory subregions whose summation properties accounted for orientation tuning; complex if no subregion structure was detectable yet the cell was orientation-tuned and largely position-invariant within the field; hypercomplex if the cell was orientation-tuned and showed end-stopping (response decreasing for stimuli extending beyond a critical length).

**Binocular protocols and ocular dominance.** Stimuli were presented to each eye alone and to both eyes simultaneously, with independent control of position so that binocular configuration could be varied. Each cell was assigned to one of seven ocular-dominance categories (1 = exclusively contralateral, 4 = equal, 7 = exclusively ipsilateral) as in the 1962 cat work.

**Architectural reconstruction.** From tangential penetrations across the cortical surface the authors plotted preferred orientation, receptive-field position, and ocular dominance as functions of surface coordinates; from radial penetrations they plotted the same properties as functions of cortical depth. The reconstructed maps revealed the joint orientation/ocular-dominance/retinotopic structure of macaque V1 and the laminar distribution of simple, complex, and hypercomplex cells.

**Sampling strategy.** Penetrations were distributed across the operculum of striate cortex, sampling parafoveal as well as more peripheral visual-field representations. The two-species design (macaque and spider monkey) was deliberate: cross-species consistency was used as evidence that observed features are generally primate rather than species-specific. Across penetrations the authors accumulated enough cells to make population-level statements about laminar specialisation, column geometry, and the joint orientation × ocular-dominance map, even though the receptive-field maps themselves are presented as exemplar cases rather than averaged tuning curves.

## 5. Results

- **Sample.** Several hundred cells from many macaque and spider monkey penetrations, sampling all cortical layers and crossing multiple orientation columns and ocular-dominance bands.
- **Receptive-field categories.** Simple, complex, and hypercomplex cells all present in macaque V1, with the same qualitative properties described in cat (Hubel & Wiesel 1962, 1965). The simple → complex → hypercomplex hierarchy is laid down anatomically across cortical layers.
- **Receptive-field sizes.** Substantially smaller than in cat, particularly near the fovea. Foveal receptive fields can be a small fraction of a degree of visual angle; peripheral fields are larger but still smaller than equivalent-eccentricity cat fields.
- **Orientation tuning.** Sharper than in cat, with many cells showing strong nulls at orientations 20–30° off preferred. The 180° orientation space is sampled densely by the population.
- **Orientation columns.** Radial penetrations encounter cells of nearly the same preferred orientation throughout cortical depth, with orientation preference varying smoothly across tangential surface penetrations in steps of approximately 10–20° between adjacent columns. The macaque orientation-column system is qualitatively similar to cat but with possibly narrower column spacing matching the smaller receptive fields.
- **Ocular-dominance columns.** Alternating bands of left-eye and right-eye dominance run across the cortical surface, with band widths of roughly 0.25–0.5 mm. Layer IV (the geniculate-recipient layer) shows the strongest segregation; cells in supragranular and infragranular layers are mostly binocular, with the eye-of-origin label retained but weaker.
- **Independence of the two column systems.** Orientation preference varies smoothly across the cortical surface independently of ocular dominance: within each ocular-dominance band, orientation cycles through its full 180° range; across band boundaries orientation preference is preserved while eye-of-origin flips. The result is a two-dimensional joint map of orientation × eye-of-origin overlaid on the retinotopic position map.
- **Laminar organisation.** Layers IVA and IVB are dominated by simple cells, most monocularly driven, reflecting the unmixed geniculate input. Some orientation-non-specific units (presumably geniculate afferents or stellate cells receiving them) are also found here. Layers II, III, V, and VI contain predominantly complex and some hypercomplex cells, most of which are binocularly driven; these reflect the intracortical processing that builds higher-order receptive-field properties from the layer-IV simple-cell input.
- **Direction-of-motion selectivity.** Present especially among complex cells; the strength and prevalence of direction selectivity varies regionally across the cortical surface.
- **Colour-coded cells.** A small minority of cells respond differentially to wavelength as well as to spatial configuration, foreshadowing the cytochrome-oxidase blob compartments that subsequent work (Livingstone & Hubel 1984, 1988) mapped in detail.
- **Retinotopy and cortical magnification.** The visual field is mapped onto V1 with the central several degrees occupying a disproportionately large area of cortex; receptive-field position varies smoothly across the cortical surface, with the magnification factor falling off with eccentricity.
- **Cross-species replication.** Findings in spider monkey were qualitatively identical to those in macaque, indicating that the simple/complex/hypercomplex hierarchy, the orientation columns, and the ocular-dominance bands are general primate features rather than macaque-specific specialisations.
- **Joint encoding over the cortical sheet.** The combination of retinotopy on the surface, orientation preference varying smoothly across the surface, ocular-dominance bands running orthogonally, and motion-direction varying regionally produces a *multi-dimensional functional map* in which each cortical surface location encodes a tuple (visual-field position, preferred orientation, eye-of-origin preference, motion-direction preference, with a small colour-coded subpopulation). The 1968 paper is the first reported joint map of this many feature dimensions in primate cortex.
- **Hypercolumn precursor.** The combination of one full cycle of orientation preferences (a complete 180° rotation) with one full cycle of ocular dominance (one left-eye band + one right-eye band) over a small surface region of cortex is the structure that Hubel & Wiesel later formalised as the *hypercolumn* — the unit of cortex that contains a representation of every orientation and both eye-of-origin labels at one retinotopic location. The 1968 paper documents the data from which the hypercolumn concept was synthesised even though the term is not yet used here.
- **Quantitative cat-vs.-monkey contrast.** The authors note explicitly that comparable cells in macaque vs. cat differ in receptive-field size (smaller in macaque, particularly near the fovea) and in orientation-tuning sharpness (sharper in macaque), consistent with primate fovea's higher acuity. They also note the existence of ocular-dominance columns as a clearer and more strongly segregated structure in monkey than in cat, where ocular-dominance organisation was only hinted at in 1962.
- **Penetration-by-penetration consistency.** Across both species and across many penetrations, the same architectural picture emerged repeatedly: radial penetrations encountered orientation-homogeneous columns; tangential penetrations encountered smooth orientation progressions punctuated by ocular-dominance-band boundaries; cells of a given layer had cell-type properties consistent with the laminar specialisation noted. The reproducibility across animals and penetrations is itself part of what made the result definitive for the field.
- **Comparison to cat.** Across every property compared, the cat-vs.-macaque difference is in the direction of *finer-grained* primate computation: smaller foveal receptive fields, sharper orientation tuning, additional separation of monocular and binocular processing through ocular-dominance columns, and a stronger laminar specialisation of cell type. The cat work is qualitatively predictive of the primate result, but the primate result is the more demanding empirical target that any model of human or primate-derived AI vision must match.
- **Confidence in conclusions.** The combined evidence across hundreds of cells, multiple animals of two primate species, and the consistency of orientation and ocular-dominance maps inferred independently from radial and tangential penetrations gave the paper the empirical weight to be taken as definitive within the field — the foundation on which decades of primate-V1 work was subsequently built.

## 6. Critique / limitations

- The receptive-field maps are still **hand-drawn from spot stimulation** rather than computed from reverse correlation; the simple/complex/hypercomplex taxonomy is qualitative. Modern white-noise and natural-image methods recover a continuum of receptive-field properties and reveal nonlinearities (gain control, cross-orientation suppression, end-stopping gradients) that the 1968 methods cannot isolate (Skottun et al. 1991; Carandini & Heeger 2012).
- **All recordings are from lightly anaesthetised animals.** Awake primate V1 shows substantially more dynamic, attention-modulated, and recurrence-shaped responses (Reynolds & Heeger 2009; Gilbert & Li 2013) that the 1968 paradigm cannot reveal. The classical receptive field reported in 1968 is the *feedforward-driven* component of a much richer recurrent computation.
- The paper is **silent on contextual modulation**: surround suppression, contour integration, figure-ground modulation, and other extra-classical effects that subsequent work establishes as load-bearing for V1 computation are not addressed.
- **Optical-imaging refinements** (Blasdel & Salama 1986; Bonhoeffer & Grinvald 1991; Blasdel 1992) revealed that orientation preference forms **pinwheel** singularities at which preferred orientation cycles through 180° in a small region — a structure invisible to single-electrode penetrations. The orientation map of 1968 is a low-resolution projection of the pinwheel architecture later established.
- The **cytochrome-oxidase blob system** (Livingstone & Hubel 1984), the **parvo/magno/konio segregation** at the LGN and its layer-IV projections (Livingstone & Hubel 1988), and the **CO-blob → V2 thin-stripe / interstripe / thick-stripe** projection topology (Sincich & Horton 2005) are all absent or barely hinted at in the 1968 paper. The colour-coded cells noted in passing become a major sub-system in subsequent work.
- The paper supports a **strictly feedforward** picture: geniculate → layer-IV simple → supragranular/infragranular complex → hypercomplex. The role of **intracortical recurrence**, **layer-6 corticocortical feedback** (Weiler et al. 2025), and **top-down feedback** from V2, V4, and IT is not addressed; modern accounts (Kietzmann et al. 2019, in seed; Bastos et al. 2012, in seed) make recurrence and top-down feedback mandatory for matching primate V1 dynamics.
- **No quantitative model** is fit to the data; the hierarchical pooling account is verbal and graphical. The energy-model formalisation of the complex cell (Adelson & Bergen 1985) and the Gabor-function parameterisation of the simple cell (Jones & Palmer 1987) come later.
- The **species comparison cat vs. macaque** is qualitative, not quantitative; specific differences in column geometry, receptive-field size distributions, and orientation-tuning sharpness across the two species are noted but not characterised quantitatively. Later comparative work (De Valois & De Valois 1988) provides the quantitative comparison.
- The **anatomical resolution of the laminar findings** rests on Nissl-based reconstruction of penetration tracks, which is coarser than the layer-specific markers later available (cytochrome oxidase histochemistry, layer-specific immunolabelling). Some assignments of cells to specific sublayers (especially within layer IV's IVA/IVB/IVC subdivisions) were refined in subsequent work, and the subdivision of layer IVC into IVCα (magnocellular-recipient) and IVCβ (parvocellular-recipient) — central to the modern primate-V1 picture — is anticipated only loosely in the 1968 account.
- **Population statistics on receptive-field size, orientation-tuning sharpness, and ocular-dominance distribution** are presented qualitatively or as exemplar cases rather than as histograms with confidence intervals. The cat-vs.-monkey comparison in particular would benefit from a paired quantitative analysis, which the 1968 dataset's exemplar-focused presentation does not provide.
- The **CO-blob-vs.-interblob distinction** within superficial layers (Livingstone & Hubel 1984) is absent — the 1968 paper notes only a small colour-coded subpopulation without recognising it as a spatially-organised compartment with its own projection topology to V2 thin stripes.

## 7. Connection to our work

This is the **primate empirical anchor** for every V1-level commitment in the user's architectural program. The 1962 cat paper established the receptive-field-and-column framework; the 1968 macaque paper proves that the same framework holds in the species directly relevant to human and primate-derived AI. Combined, they form the empirical bedrock for V1 inductive biases in CNNs and ViTs.

**Primate-aligned architectural priors vs. domain-general transformers.** A central commitment of the user's program is that *getting the wiring right* matters in a way that scale alone cannot substitute for (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md` §3, "Wiring is computation"). The 1968 paper licenses that commitment in the primate-specific case: macaque V1 has smaller receptive fields, sharper orientation tuning, an independent ocular-dominance column system, and a systematic laminar specialisation that the 1962 cat work could only partially anticipate. These primate-specific features are exactly the kind of architectural inductive biases that the user's program embraces — the patch-token grid, the multi-channel feature representation, the layer-by-layer abstraction of the GridCell-RNN stack (program §2, §3) are all expressions of primate-cortical organisation, not arbitrary deep-learning design choices.

**Simple/complex/hypercomplex hierarchy → multi-stage encoder.** The 1968 confirmation that macaque V1 instantiates the simple → complex → hypercomplex hierarchy across cortical layers is the empirical foundation for the multi-stage encoder in PRISM v1 / v2 / the user's GridCell-RNN stack (program §3). PRISM v1's hierarchical conv backbone (`THESIS.md` §2.4) and the user's three-layer reference design (program §3, Layers 1, 2, 3) directly mirror the macaque laminar progression: small-RF, orientation-tuned simple-cell-like features at the lowest layer; larger-RF, position-invariant complex-cell-like features at intermediate layers; end-stopped, contour-segment-selective hypercomplex-cell-like features at the highest layer. The empirical justification for stacking these stages comes from the 1968 paper's demonstration that primate cortex literally does this.

**Orientation columns and joint feature maps → patch-token representations.** The 1968 paper's central architectural finding — that orientation and eye-of-origin are mapped **independently and orthogonally** over the cortical surface — is the deepest biological precedent for the multi-channel patch-token representation used by ViTs and by the user's Feedback Transformer (program §1). In primate V1, a single retinotopic location is associated with a vector of feature labels (orientation, eye-of-origin, motion-direction, colour-coded vs. broadband); the cortical sheet implements this jointly with the retinotopic position map. The ViT's patch-token vector at a given grid position carries an analogous multi-feature representation. The user's GridCell-RNN (program §2) is even more literal: a grid of recurrent cells, each implementing spatially-independent processing followed by Feedback-Transformer integration, is the artificial analogue of the cortical orientation-column architecture documented in 1968.

**Ocular-dominance columns → parallel-feedback / multi-modal integration.** The 1968 paper's demonstration that primate cortex implements **alternating bands of monocular dominance** with substantial *binocular* integration in supragranular and infragranular layers is the cellular precedent for the user's commitment to integrating multiple parallel input streams via the Feedback Transformer (program §1). Each macaque V1 hypercolumn integrates left- and right-eye information through intracortical wiring; the Feedback Transformer integrates multiple parallel and hierarchical memory streams through Q/K/V projection and broadcasting. The integration *is* a computation, as Hubel & Wiesel emphasise — disparity-based depth, in macaque, is built in cortex by binocular integration, not inherited from subcortical inputs. The user's program asserts the same principle at the level of memory-feedback integration: getting the integration mechanism right (Q/K/V projections per stream, element-wise broadcasting before softmax) is what allows the system to compute over multiple input sources.

**Laminar specialisation → descending-projection conv stack.** The 1968 paper's clean separation between **layer IV** (simple/monocular, geniculate-input-dominated) and **supragranular/infragranular layers** (complex/hypercomplex, binocular, intracortically driven) is the biological template for the layer-by-layer abstraction of the descending-projection conv stack of the GridCell-RNN stack (program §3, "Descending Projections"). The descending projection's reducing-spatial-resolution / expanding-channel-count architecture is precisely the kind of transformation that the 1968 laminar progression implements: layer IV's monocular, simple, smaller-RF representation is replaced in supragranular layers by binocular, complex, larger-RF representations that pool over more retinotopic space and span more feature dimensions.

**Companion to the 1962 cat paper.** The 1962 and 1968 papers function as a coupled empirical foundation: 1962 establishes the architecture; 1968 establishes its primate generality. For any architectural claim the user's program makes about V1 (oriented receptive fields, hierarchical pooling, columnar organisation, retinotopy), the 1962 paper provides the original demonstration and the 1968 paper provides the primate-confirmed version. The user's commitment to primate-aligned architectural priors (program §3, §5) over domain-general transformer scale is justified precisely by the existence of this 1968 confirmation — without it, the cat data would be a suggestive but species-misaligned starting point.

**Connection to downstream literature.** The 1968 paper feeds directly into Felleman & Van Essen 1991 (`felleman_vanessen1991_hierarchical_cortex`, in seed), which catalogues the full hierarchical structure of macaque visual cortex of which V1 is the entry point; into Tanaka 1996 (`tanaka1996_it_object_vision`, in seed), which characterises the ventral-stream endpoint (IT) where the orientation-tuned features of V1 have been pooled and abstracted into object selectivity; into Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999 (`riesenhuber_poggio1999_hierarchical_models`, in seed), whose HMAX is the explicit computational descendant of the simple/complex hierarchy in primate V1 and downstream visual areas; and into DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust 2012 (`dicarlo2012_object_recognition`, in seed), the modern review of the primate ventral stream that takes the 1968 architecture as its starting point. The user's program inherits the entire chain: V1 receptive fields (1962/1968) → hierarchical pooling (HMAX) → ventral-stream endpoint (IT) → modern DNN models of object recognition (DiCarlo 2012). Each link in this chain is empirically anchored in primate neurophysiology, and 1968 is the foundational primate link without which the chain would terminate at the cat-V1 stage.

**Justifying the architectural-bias commitment against the "scale is all you need" position.** A modern objection to the user's program is that domain-general transformers, given enough scale, will eventually approximate any required visual computation without explicit V1-style inductive biases. The 1962/1968 pair is the strongest single empirical argument against that position in the visual domain: real biological visual systems, having had hundreds of millions of years and enormous metabolic / bandwidth pressure to find efficient solutions, converge on a specific architecture (orientation columns, ocular-dominance bands, retinotopy, simple → complex → hypercomplex hierarchy, laminar specialisation, multi-feature joint maps) rather than a generic high-dimensional embedding. The user's program reads this convergence as evidence that *architecture matters* in a way that scale alone does not capture, and the 1968 primate confirmation is what removes the species-translation objection to that reading.

**Independence of feature dimensions → multi-channel patch-token vectors.** The 1968 paper's demonstration that orientation, ocular dominance, motion direction, and (for a subpopulation) colour are mapped *independently* over the cortical surface is the architectural precedent for treating a patch token's channel dimension as a *factored* feature space rather than a generic high-dimensional embedding. The user's program does not yet exploit this fully — most ViT-style patch projections in the published Recurrent ViT and PRISM systems use a single linear projection per patch — but the multi-channel feature-map commitments of the Feedback Transformer (program §1, per-state Q/K/V projections) point in this direction. A natural future extension is to factor the patch-token channels into a small number of *named* feature dimensions (orientation, motion, eye-of-origin, colour) explicitly, exactly as primate V1 does.

**Sharper tuning and smaller RFs → favourable for ViT-style patch tokenisation.** A *quantitative* lesson of the 1968 paper that is easy to miss: primate receptive fields are smaller and orientation tuning is sharper than in cat. For the user's program, this is the empirically-favourable direction: a ViT-style architecture that tokenises an image into small patches (the user's patch grid is typically 8×8 or 16×16 pixels) corresponds to assigning each patch a representation built from a population of cells whose receptive fields are of comparable spatial extent to the patch itself. In macaque foveal V1, with receptive fields a small fraction of a degree across, the *biological* patch size is exactly the right scale for the artificial patch grid the user's program operates over. The cat-only data would leave the patch-size choice less well-grounded; the 1968 primate data closes that gap.

**Direction-of-motion selectivity → video / change-detection work.** The 1968 paper documents that direction-of-motion selectivity is present in macaque complex cells, with regional variation across the cortical surface. This is the V1-level substrate for the motion-sensitive computations that PRISM and the Recurrent ViT exploit in change-detection (Recurrent ViT paper §6) and video autoencoding (program §6, "Video VAE"). A V1-level layer that supplies motion-direction-selective features to higher layers is biologically grounded in the 1968 macaque data, not just in cat.

**Limits and what we do not inherit.** Like the 1962 paper, the 1968 paper supports a **strictly feedforward** account of V1: orientation tuning emerges from geniculate convergence to layer-IV simple cells; position invariance emerges from simple → complex pooling; no role is given to recurrent or top-down computation. The user's program rejects this asymmetry and commits to bidirectional hierarchical feedback at every level (program §3), following Kietzmann et al. 2019 (in seed), Rao & Ballard 1999 (in seed), Friston 2010 (in seed), and Bastos et al. 2012 (in seed). The classical primate V1 of Hubel & Wiesel is, in the user's view, the *feedforward kernel* of a much richer recurrent computation that the 1968 paradigm could not access. The colour-coded subpopulation noted in passing, and the laminar specialisation that the paper documents, are also points where modern primate-V1 work (Livingstone & Hubel 1984, 1988; Sincich & Horton 2005) substantially refines the 1968 picture in ways the user's program could productively follow up. In particular, the eventual integration of a colour / form / motion functional segregation into a primate-aligned multi-channel patch representation — already hinted at by the 1968 colour-coded subpopulation and the direction-selective complex cells — is a natural extension of the user's multi-channel feature-map commitments (program §1) that the present architecture does not yet exploit.

- `hubel_wiesel1959_single_unit_cat` — the 1959 *J. Physiol.* paper, the predecessor of the 1962 and 1968 receptive-field papers. Not yet in seed.
- `mountcastle1957_columnar_somatosensory` — Mountcastle's original discovery of columnar organisation in somatosensory cortex, the conceptual precursor of the orientation and ocular-dominance column findings. Not yet in seed.
- `hubel_wiesel1977_ferrier_lecture` — the Ferrier Lecture (Proc. R. Soc. B 198:1-59) consolidating the macaque V1 architecture and adding hypercolumn and ice-cube-model synthesis. Not yet in seed.
- `livingstone_hubel1984_anatomy_physiology_blobs` — the cytochrome-oxidase blob system in macaque V1, refining the colour-coded subpopulation noted in 1968. Not yet in seed.
- `livingstone_hubel1988_segregation_form_color` — magno/parvo segregation and the dorsal/ventral split downstream of V1; Hubel's later synthesis of the primate visual streams. Not yet in seed.
- `blasdel_salama1986_optical_imaging` — optical imaging of orientation maps in macaque V1, revealing the pinwheel architecture invisible to single-electrode penetrations. Not yet in seed.
- `bonhoeffer_grinvald1991_pinwheel` — pinwheel architecture of orientation maps. Not yet in seed.
- `sincich_horton2005_v1_v2_stripes` — the CO-blob → V2 thin/interstripe/thick-stripe projection topology that extends the 1968 column architecture into V2. Not yet in seed.
- `adelson_bergen1985_energy_model` — quantitative energy-model formalisation of the primate complex cell. Not yet in seed.
- `jones_palmer1987_gabor_receptive_fields` — Gabor-function fits to primate simple-cell receptive fields; the modern parametric form. Not yet in seed.
- `de_valois_de_valois1988_spatial_vision` — the quantitative comparative account of cat vs. primate spatial vision extending Hubel & Wiesel 1962/1968. Not yet in seed.
- `carandini_heeger2012_normalization` — divisive normalisation as the canonical V1 nonlinearity that the 1968 hand-mapping methods could not isolate. Not yet in seed.
- `gilbert_li2013_top_down_visual` — top-down modulation of V1, complicating the strict feedforward account. Cited in user's notes; not yet in seed.
- `fukushima1980_neocognitron` — the first explicit hierarchical artificial network derived from Hubel & Wiesel's simple/complex hierarchy in primate V1. Not yet in seed.
- `lecun1998_lenet` — convolutional networks that explicitly invoke the simple/complex hierarchy as architectural motivation. Not yet in seed.
- `hubel_wiesel1974_uniformity_monkey_v1` — the follow-up *J. Comp. Neurol.* paper quantifying the uniformity of orientation column geometry across the macaque V1 sheet. Not yet in seed.
- `tootell1988_v1_topography_macaque` — quantitative topography of macaque V1 obtained with 2-deoxyglucose autoradiography, refining the retinotopic and column-geometry findings of 1968. Not yet in seed.
- `obermayer_blasdel1993_geometric_orientation_maps` — formal characterisation of the geometric layout of primate orientation columns (pinwheels, linear zones), which subsumes the 1968 surface-orientation findings. Not yet in seed.
- `horton_hocking1996_intrinsic_ocular_dominance` — quantitative geometry of macaque ocular-dominance columns, completing the 1968 sketch with precise band-width and topology measurements. Not yet in seed.
- `wiesel_hubel1963_visual_deprivation` — the deprivation experiments in kittens showing experience-dependent plasticity of ocular dominance, complementary to the adult-anatomy findings of 1968. Not yet in seed.
- `daugman1985_uncertainty_gabor` — Gabor functions as optimal joint space-frequency representations; theoretical justification for V1 receptive-field shape. Not yet in seed.
- `field1987_relations_v1_natural_statistics` — natural-image statistics arguments for the optimality of V1 oriented receptive fields. Not yet in seed.

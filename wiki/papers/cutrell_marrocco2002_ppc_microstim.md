# Electrical microstimulation of primate posterior parietal cortex initiates orienting and alerting components of covert attention

## 1. Abstract

Cutrell and Marrocco (2002) tested the causal contribution of primate posterior parietal cortex (PPC) to the components of covert visuospatial attention identified by the Posner cuing paradigm. The PPC has long been implicated in the control of visuospatial orienting, encompassing both overt saccadic eye movements and covert attentional shifts to peripheral locations independent of gaze. The authors trained rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) on a spatial cued target-detection task in which the animals maintained central fixation throughout the trial — no eye movement was permitted — and responded to a peripheral target preceded by a peripheral visual cue. On a subset of trials, electrical microstimulation was delivered to the right PPC during the cue-target interval. At high currents, microstimulation evoked contralateral saccades, as expected from earlier mapping work. Critically, with currents reduced below the saccade-evocation threshold, the same stimulation produced a purely attentional effect: reaction times to contralateral targets were faster on stimulation than on no-stimulation trials, mimicking the cuing benefit produced by a valid peripheral cue. In addition, a smaller but reliable reaction-time reduction was observed on trials where the stimulation could not be plausibly attributed to spatial orienting toward the target location — a generalized arousal or alerting effect riding on top of the spatial orienting effect. Together, the results establish that PPC microstimulation initiates two of the three classical components of attention identified by Posner — orienting (spatially specific) and alerting (spatially nonspecific) — without necessarily evoking a motor act. The PPC is therefore not merely a sensorimotor relay but a source of both the spatial-priority signal that biases selection and the gain signal that controls overall behavioral readiness.

## 2. Why this matters for us

This is the earliest of the parietal causal-perturbation papers cited in the user's notes, and it supplies a crucial dissociation that the more recent Mirpour et al. 2010 entry does not: PPC microstimulation produces *both* a spatially specific orienting effect *and* a spatially nonspecific alerting effect. That two-component decomposition aligns directly with the sensitivity/bias decomposition of attention later developed by Sridharan et al. 2017 and Luo & Maunsell 2018 ([sridharan2017_sc_sensitivity_bias](research_db/papers/sridharan2017_sc_sensitivity_bias.md), [luo_maunsell2018_criterion_sensitivity](research_db/papers/luo_maunsell2018_criterion_sensitivity.md)) and with the dual mechanisms — multiplicative spatial gain plus additive baseline shift — that any single self-attention map produces when perturbed. The recurrent ViT's attention-perturbation experiments should therefore be expected to produce *both* a spatially local selection bias *and* a global gain effect on response rate, exactly as Cutrell & Marrocco observed in PPC. This paper is the parietal anchor for that two-effect prediction.

It also provides a direct precedent for the recurrent ViT's perturbation methodology by demonstrating that subthreshold electrical stimulation of a priority-map substrate is sufficient to bias covert attention without driving an overt motor act. The recurrent ViT's analog — biasing the self-attention map without overriding the decoder's selection — is the in-silico realization of the same experimental logic. Together with Moore & Armstrong (2003, FEF), Cavanaugh & Wurtz (2004, SC), and Mirpour et al. (2010, LIP free search), Cutrell & Marrocco completes a four-paper neurophysiological precedent for the recurrent ViT's perturbation experiments, with Cutrell & Marrocco supplying both the parietal causal anchor *and* the orienting-vs-alerting decomposition that the other three papers do not separately report.

## 3. Key claims

1. Subthreshold electrical microstimulation of primate PPC, delivered during the cue-target interval of a Posner-style covert-attention task, reduces reaction times to peripheral targets in the contralateral visual field — a behavioral signature of spatial orienting.
2. The reduction in contralateral RT occurs without any overt eye movement: the monkeys maintain fixation throughout the trial, and currents are titrated below the saccade-evocation threshold.
3. Above the saccade-evocation threshold, the same PPC sites produce contralateral saccades — consistent with PPC's status as a sensorimotor area whose output addresses both attention and saccades.
4. A separate, smaller reaction-time reduction is observed on trials where stimulation cannot be attributed to spatial orienting toward the target. This indicates a generalized alerting effect — a tonic increase in behavioral readiness across the visual field — superimposed on the spatially specific orienting effect.
5. The two effects (orienting and alerting) correspond to two of the three components of attention identified by Posner & Petersen (1990): a spatial orienting network and an arousal/alerting network. PPC microstimulation engages both.
6. PPC is therefore a source of both attentional priority and general arousal signals, not merely a sensorimotor transformation. The same neural substrate that biases selection toward a contralateral location also modulates global behavioral readiness.
7. The orienting and alerting components are dissociable in the data: their magnitudes do not co-vary perfectly across sites, and their behavioral signatures (spatial specificity vs. spatial nonspecificity) are distinct.
8. Below the saccade-evocation threshold, the same physical stimulus that would otherwise drive an overt eye movement produces a purely covert attentional effect — establishing that covert attention and overt saccades share neural machinery in PPC but can be dissociated by current intensity.
9. The chronometric (RT) signature of stimulation-induced orienting matches that of a valid peripheral cue on no-stim trials, suggesting that the PPC stimulation injects a signal functionally equivalent to a normal exogenous attentional cue.

## 4. Methods

**Subjects.** Rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta), trained over months on a peripheral-cued detection task with sustained central fixation.

**Task.** A spatial cued target-detection paradigm. Each trial began with the monkey fixating a central spot. After a fixation period, a peripheral visual cue appeared briefly at one of several locations in the left or right visual field. After a variable cue-target interval, a peripheral target appeared at one of the cued or uncued locations. The animal was rewarded for a manual or saccadic response to the target while maintaining central fixation up to the moment of response. Critically, gaze had to remain at the center fixation point throughout the cue and during the cue-target interval — gaze break aborted the trial. The reaction time from target onset to response was the primary dependent measure.

**Microstimulation protocol.** A microelectrode was advanced into right PPC at sites identified by the local single-unit visual and saccade-related activity. Two regimes were tested: (a) high currents that reliably evoked contralateral saccades by stimulation alone, used to confirm site mapping; (b) sub-saccade-threshold currents — below the level required to evoke an eye movement — delivered during the cue-target interval. Pulse trains were biphasic at the standard parameters used in primate microstimulation studies. Stimulation was time-locked to the post-cue, pre-target window, so any behavioral effect operated on the same attention-allocation process as the visual cue itself.

**Design.** Stimulation trials were interleaved with no-stimulation trials, and trials varied in cue validity (valid, invalid, neutral) and in target location (contralateral vs. ipsilateral to the stimulated hemisphere). The orthogonal comparison — RT on stim vs. no-stim, conditioned on target location relative to stimulated hemisphere — gives the orienting effect. The comparison — RT on stim vs. no-stim, on trials where the target is in the ipsilateral field (where spatial orienting cannot account for facilitation) — isolates the alerting component.

**Stimulation timing window.** Stimulation was time-locked to the cue-target interval, the temporal window during which endogenous attention allocation is occurring on normal trials. This timing locks the perturbation onto the same processing stage that the visual cue itself targets — the allocation of spatial priority prior to target detection. Stimulation outside this window (e.g., during the inter-trial interval, or after target onset) would in principle dissociate the alerting effect (which would still operate, since arousal can be raised at any time) from the orienting effect (which requires the stimulation to coincide with the attention-allocation window). The 2002 paper focuses on stimulation within the cue-target window; off-window stimulation is a methodological follow-up that the in-silico recurrent ViT analog can easily implement by varying the recurrent-step index at which the perturbation is injected.

**Within-session controls.** Stim and no-stim trials were interleaved within each recording session and matched in cue validity, target location, and trial position so that any session-level drift in arousal or motivation contributed equally to both conditions. The orthogonal comparison of stim vs no-stim trials within matched cue and target conditions therefore isolates the stimulation's effect from session-level confounds. Catch trials (no target) confirmed that the animal was withholding response rather than producing premature responses on stim trials, ruling out a pure motor-impulsivity account of the RT reductions.

**Controls.** Currents were titrated for each site to remain below saccade-evocation threshold. Trials with broken fixation were excluded. The basic dependent variable — RT — controls for many alternative interpretations: stimulation that altered visual processing (detection or discrimination) would manifest as accuracy changes rather than RT shifts; stimulation that altered motor preparation alone would not show spatial specificity. The combination of spatially specific RT facilitation plus a smaller, spatially generic RT facilitation is the signature of a dual mechanism.

**Threshold titration.** A key methodological move is the per-site titration of stimulation current. Each electrode site was first characterized at currents sufficient to evoke saccades, mapping the contralateral endpoint and confirming the site's location in the eye-movement-related portion of PPC. Currents were then reduced incrementally until saccades were no longer evoked on any trial, and the attentional protocol was run at this sub-evocation current. This per-site current titration is the analog of the recurrent ViT's choice of perturbation magnitude: too large a perturbation overrides the model's selection mechanism (the equivalent of motor capture), while too small a perturbation produces no behavioral effect. The narrow window in which a perturbation biases without overriding is the operating regime that primate microstimulation studies and the recurrent ViT's analog experiments both target.

## 5. Results

The principal quantitative findings:

- **Saccade evocation at high currents.** Above threshold, PPC microstimulation reliably produced contralateral saccades whose endpoint depended on the stimulated site's retinotopic mapping. This confirmed that the sites were in the eye-movement-related PPC subregion (the macaque homologue of LIP and surrounding areas) and provided the threshold reference for the attentional protocol.
- **Subthreshold RT facilitation on contralateral trials.** With currents reduced below the saccade-evocation threshold, RT on contralateral-target trials was faster on stimulation trials than on no-stimulation trials. The magnitude of the facilitation was on the order of tens of milliseconds — comparable to the cuing benefit produced by a valid peripheral cue. This is the *orienting* component: stimulation acts as if it were a peripheral attentional cue.
- **Alerting effect on non-orienting trials.** On trials where stimulation could not be construed as spatial orienting toward the target — for example, ipsilateral targets, or targets at locations far from the stimulated RF — a smaller RT reduction was nonetheless observed. This is the *alerting* component: a generalized increase in behavioral readiness, spatially nonspecific.
- **Dissociation between orienting and alerting.** The two effects, while both arising from PPC stimulation, are distinguishable by their spatial profiles. The orienting effect is contralaterally selective and tracks the stimulated site's RF; the alerting effect is spatially diffuse and rides across both visual fields. Their magnitudes and dependence on stimulation parameters differ.
- **No accuracy disruption.** The animals' detection accuracy was not impaired by stimulation; only RT was modulated. This rules out accounts in which stimulation degraded visual processing or motor execution.
- **No motor capture.** Below threshold, no saccades were evoked; the animals maintained central fixation throughout. The RT effects therefore operate on covert attention rather than on overt orienting.

The combination — spatially selective RT facilitation + spatially nonspecific RT facilitation + no accuracy change + no motor capture — is the signature of a dual-component perturbation: PPC injects both a spatial-priority signal and a global gain/arousal signal into downstream selection.

A further qualitative observation is that the spatial-orienting effect of PPC stimulation mimics the RT signature of a *valid peripheral cue* on no-stim trials — i.e., the manipulation is behaviorally indistinguishable, at the level of RT, from giving the animal an extra exogenous spatial cue. This is the strongest possible evidence that the PPC signal is a *priority* signal as conventionally defined: a representation whose elevated activity at one location is read out by the selection machinery as if the location were extrinsically cued. The alerting component, by contrast, has no spatial cue analog — it is the chronometric signature of an *intrinsically* generated arousal state, of the kind associated with phasic locus coeruleus activity or with the ascending cholinergic system. PPC microstimulation therefore engages both an *extrinsic-cue-like* spatial signal and an *intrinsic-arousal-like* generalized signal from a single physical perturbation, which is the empirical foundation for treating these two signals as outputs of a single substrate rather than two anatomically separate networks.

## 6. Critique / limitations

The paper does not separate the two components mechanistically. The dissociation is behavioral — spatial selectivity vs. nonselectivity — but the underlying neural source could in principle be the same population of PPC neurons signaling in two readout modes, or two distinct populations interleaved at the stimulated site. Microstimulation cannot distinguish these without complementary recording or inactivation work. The subsequent neurophysiological literature (Bisley & Goldberg 2010 priority-map review; Mirpour et al. 2010 free-search microstimulation) addresses parts of this question, but the population-level decomposition of orienting vs. alerting signals within PPC is not fully resolved.

Electrical microstimulation activates fibers of passage in addition to local cell bodies. The PPC stimulation site is anatomically adjacent to many ascending and descending fiber tracts (LIP-FEF, LIP-SC, LIP-pulvinar), so the spatial-orienting effect could in principle be mediated by activation of LIP-to-SC or LIP-to-FEF afferents rather than by local LIP processing. The alerting effect is more suspect on this count: a generalized arousal signal could reflect activation of ascending arousal pathways (locus coeruleus afferents, basal forebrain projections) passing through or near the stimulated region. Subsequent pharmacological inactivation and optogenetic work has partially refined the localization, but the original microstimulation paradigm has this caveat.

The task is a peripheral-cued detection paradigm, the classical Posner design. The orienting effect is therefore measured against a relatively narrow behavioral regime — one in which spatial cues drive endogenous-like or exogenous-like attention, but not in which the animal engages in free visual search (the Mirpour 2010 paradigm) or in change-blindness paradigms (the Cavanaugh & Wurtz 2004 paradigm). Whether the dual orienting/alerting signature generalizes to these other paradigms is not directly tested here.

Only one hemisphere is stimulated at a time. The within-trial comparison (contralateral vs. ipsilateral target) controls for nonspecific drift in arousal across the session, but a bilateral or contralateral-control stimulation design would more cleanly separate strictly spatial from strictly arousal components. The reliance on RT (a chronometric measure that integrates multiple processing stages) rather than psychophysical sensitivity/criterion separation also limits the inferential precision — the Sridharan and Luo-Maunsell sensitivity/bias decomposition was not yet available in 2002.

The sample is small (a handful of animals, dozens of sites), as is standard for primate microstimulation work. The alerting effect, in particular, is reported as a smaller and more variable finding than the orienting effect; its replication in subsequent independent studies would strengthen the dissociation claim. The 2002 paper is thus best understood as the *opening move* in a literature that subsequently developed via Bisley-Goldberg single-unit recording, Mirpour-Bisley free-search microstimulation, and the broader Krauzlis-Bollimunta literature on SC and FEF contributions.

The interaction between cue validity and stimulation is also not deeply parameterized. A modern follow-up would systematically vary the stimulation-cue temporal interval, cue validity proportions, and target-distractor configurations to map the full state space of stimulation-attention interactions. The 2002 design samples this space sparsely; this is a methodological limitation that the in-silico recurrent ViT analog can address comprehensively, because perturbation parameters in the model can be swept over a dense grid at low cost.

A subtler interpretive question: the alerting component could be confounded with non-specific motor priming. If PPC stimulation increases readiness to make any motor response (manual or saccadic), the RT reduction on non-orienting trials might reflect motor preparation rather than perceptual alerting. The authors discuss this; the canonical attentional-network framework (Posner & Petersen 1990) treats alerting as a perceptual/cognitive state, but disentangling perceptual alerting from motor priming requires task designs that vary the response modality independently — work that comes later in the cited literature.

Finally, the paper does not test whether PPC's contribution to orienting is *necessary*, only that it is *sufficient* — exactly the same caveat that applies to the Mirpour et al. 2010 result. Necessity would require inactivation (muscimol or cooling) rather than stimulation. The user's program plans both kinds of manipulation in the recurrent ViT context (perturbation = sufficiency; ablation = necessity).

A separate methodological note concerns the chronometric-only readout. The dependent variable is RT, which integrates over perceptual, decisional, and motor processing stages. A reduction in RT can in principle reflect any combination of: earlier perceptual detection, faster decision accumulation, lower decision threshold, faster motor response selection, or higher motor gain. The Posner framework treats the cuing benefit as predominantly perceptual / attentional, and the absence of accuracy effects argues against a pure decision-threshold shift (which would typically trade off with errors). Nonetheless, a sequential-sampling decomposition of the RT effects — analogous to the drift-diffusion-model analyses applied to more recent attention data — would be needed to localize the orienting and alerting components within the RT-generation process. This kind of analysis is standard now but was not available in 2002. For the recurrent ViT analog, the corresponding decomposition is to inspect per-step model dynamics (attention-map evolution, decoder logits, response-rate trajectories) rather than only end-of-rollout outputs.

## 7. Connection to our work

Cutrell & Marrocco 2002 is the **earliest causal demonstration that PPC contributes to covert attention** and the only one in the user's seed that explicitly dissociates the orienting and alerting components of the perturbation. Its place in the user's program is fourfold.

**Triad-of-microstimulation extension to four.** The recurrent ViT paper (arXiv:2502.10955) and the Mirpour 2010 entry ([mirpour2010_ppc_microstim](research_db/papers/mirpour2010_ppc_microstim.md)) establish a three-paper precedent — Moore & Armstrong 2003 (FEF), Cavanaugh & Wurtz 2004 (SC), Mirpour 2010 (LIP/PPC free search) — for the perturbation methodology. Cutrell & Marrocco 2002 supplies the *cued attention* version of the parietal precedent, predating Mirpour by eight years and complementing it with a different behavioral paradigm. The four-paper precedent now spans both *cued* and *free-search* attention regimes across both *parietal* and *premotor/subcortical* substrates.

**Direct evidence for PPC's causal role complementing Bisley-Goldberg priority-map recordings.** [bisley_goldberg2010_parietal_priority](research_db/papers/bisley_goldberg2010_parietal_priority.md) establishes the priority-map account from single-unit data. Cutrell & Marrocco supplies the causal complement: perturbing PPC produces selection-like behavior, confirming that the recorded priority signal is *read out* by downstream selection. This is the same causal-from-correlative move the recurrent ViT performs internally: perturbation experiments confirm that the self-attention map is read out by the decoder, complementing the correlative observation that attention maps track ground-truth target locations.

**The orienting/alerting dissociation maps onto the sensitivity/bias decomposition.** A central methodological commitment of the user's downstream attention-perturbation experiments is to measure both spatially selective (sensitivity-like) and spatially nonselective (criterion-like, gain-like) effects of a single perturbation. The Sridharan 2017 SC-perturbation paper ([sridharan2017_sc_sensitivity_bias](research_db/papers/sridharan2017_sc_sensitivity_bias.md)) and Luo & Maunsell 2018 ([luo_maunsell2018_criterion_sensitivity](research_db/papers/luo_maunsell2018_criterion_sensitivity.md)) developed this decomposition formally. Cutrell & Marrocco's 2002 result is the parietal *precedent* for that decomposition: a single perturbation site produces *both* a spatially specific bias *and* a global gain effect. The dual-effect prediction for the recurrent ViT — that biasing one attention-map quadrant should shift both target selection (spatial bias) and overall response rate (global gain) — flows directly from this paper.

**Recurrent ViT analog: perturbing the attention map should produce both spatial-bias and global-gain effects.** The recurrent ViT's self-attention map is functionally the priority-map analog ([the_user_architectural_program](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md) §1). A graded perturbation injected at one spatial location of the map will, after softmax normalization and value aggregation, produce two distinguishable effects on downstream behavior. (i) **Local effect:** elevated attention weight at the perturbed location preferentially routes value information from that location into the readout, biasing target selection toward it — the orienting/spatial-bias analog. (ii) **Global effect:** the perturbation modifies the softmax denominator, slightly altering the gain on every other location's value contribution. If the perturbation is positive (elevated logit), other locations are relatively suppressed; if it elevates the overall mean attention weight via additive injection, all locations gain. Either way, the network's overall sensitivity / response rate is modulated alongside the spatial selection — the alerting/global-gain analog. Cutrell & Marrocco's empirical dual-effect finding is therefore the *predicted* signature of any softmax-attention perturbation, not a quirk of biology. The user's program ([the_user_architectural_program](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md) §1, [feedback_transformer](research_db/concepts/feedback_transformer.md)) inherits this prediction at every Feedback Transformer node.

**Connection to PRISM v1's prediction-error mechanism.** PRISM v1 replaces softmax attention with a prediction-error map. A spatial perturbation of the prediction-error map at one location would also be expected to produce dual effects: a spatially specific shift in which location the decoder reads out from (orienting analog), plus a global modulation of the overall prediction-error magnitude that influences gain (alerting analog). The Cutrell & Marrocco dual-component finding therefore generalizes across the softmax-attention vs. prediction-error attention divide that distinguishes the recurrent ViT from PRISM v1.

**Methodological recommendation for future P5 experiments.** The PROJECT_PLAN.md Phase 5 microstimulation-analog experiments should explicitly report *both* spatial-bias and global-gain effects, not just selection-bias. Reporting only the spatial component would miss the alerting analog that Cutrell & Marrocco identified as the second major effect of PPC microstimulation. Concretely: for each perturbation experiment, report (i) the change in selection probability at the perturbed location (orienting analog); (ii) the change in overall response rate / hit rate / RT pooled across all locations (alerting analog). The two reported jointly establish that the recurrent ViT reproduces the dual-component signature that Cutrell & Marrocco found in primate PPC.

**Threshold-titration in silico.** The titration of stimulation current — high enough to bias selection, low enough not to override it — has a direct in-silico analog. The recurrent ViT's perturbation magnitude (in logit units injected into the attention map) should be swept from zero through a regime of graded bias, into a regime of selection override. The "operating window" — the magnitude range in which perturbation biases without overriding — is the in-silico counterpart of Cutrell & Marrocco's sub-saccade-threshold current regime. Mapping the boundaries of this window and demonstrating dual orienting/alerting effects within it would close the experimental loop with the 2002 paper. The user's [feedback-transformer](research_db/concepts/feedback_transformer.md) primitive — multiplicative integration of feedback into the Q/K projections — naturally produces a graded operating window, because each feedback contribution is bounded by the magnitude of its projection rather than by an explicit threshold.

**Why this paper is more foundational than its modest citation count suggests.** Within the user's program ([the_user_architectural_program](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md)), the Cutrell & Marrocco finding is load-bearing in a way that Mirpour 2010's free-search bias is not. Mirpour establishes that PPC contributes to selection; Cutrell & Marrocco establishes that PPC contributes to *both* selection and global readiness. The dual contribution is the precise neural correlate of the dual computational role that a softmax-attention map plays — local routing plus global gain — and it justifies the architectural commitment to using a single attention substrate for both purposes rather than splitting them across two networks.

**Mapping onto Posner & Petersen (1990) tripartite framework.** Posner & Petersen famously decomposed attention into three networks: alerting (sustained readiness; norepinephrine), orienting (spatial selection; PPC, FEF, SC, pulvinar), and executive (conflict resolution; anterior cingulate, dlPFC). Cutrell & Marrocco's data show that PPC microstimulation produces effects characteristic of *two* of these three networks — orienting *and* alerting — from a single stimulation site. This is theoretically important: it suggests the canonical attentional networks are not strictly anatomically segregated but share substrate at parietal hubs. For the recurrent ViT's architecture, this maps onto the prediction that a single self-attention map can simultaneously instantiate spatial selection (orienting) and global gain (alerting) without separate machinery for each — a parsimony argument for the user's choice to centralize attention computation in one substrate rather than splitting it across modules.

**Cross-hub coalition reading.** Under the [competition-emergent-predictive-coding](research_db/concepts/competition-emergent-predictive-coding.md) framing, the alerting component is exactly what one would expect when a single coalition wins a larger share of the global attention map. The Feedback Transformer treats each hub's contribution as a multiplicative modulation of the Q/K projections; a perturbation that elevates one hub's contribution at one location simultaneously raises that hub's total softmax mass — pulling resources away from competitors at *all* locations, not just the perturbed one. The spatially specific orienting effect and the spatially nonspecific alerting effect are then two sides of one coin: the local-bias side is the explicit logit injection, the global-gain side is the implicit redistribution of softmax mass across the entire map. Cutrell & Marrocco's biological dissociation thus directly licenses the architectural prediction that perturbing one hub's contribution to the central self-attention map (the user's [multi-hub-multi-objective-system](research_db/concepts/multi_hub_multi_objective_system.md) construction) will produce dual effects with the same signature as PPC microstimulation.

**Posner tripartite framework as the behavioral readout for the recurrent ViT.** The orienting and alerting components Cutrell & Marrocco identify are two of the three components in Posner & Petersen's (1990) tripartite attention model — orienting (spatial), alerting (arousal/vigilance), and executive (conflict resolution). The recurrent ViT can be probed on all three. The change-detection task in 2502.10955 emphasizes orienting; the alerting component would be probed by, e.g., measuring response latency on stim vs no-stim trials irrespective of target location; the executive component would require a conflict task such as Eriksen flanker. The Cutrell & Marrocco template — perturb one substrate, decompose the behavioral effect into orienting and alerting — should be replicated in the recurrent ViT and its outputs reported against the Posner framework. This generalizes the perturbation analysis beyond a single behavioral measure (selection bias) to a structured space of attention components that maps directly onto the human and primate literature.

**Relation to PRISM v1's prediction-error machinery.** PRISM v1 ([THESIS.md](Prism/docs/THESIS.md) §2.4) replaces softmax attention with a prediction-error-driven selection mechanism. The same dual-component prediction applies: an artificial perturbation of the prediction-error map at one location should produce (i) a spatially specific shift in which location the decoder reads from, and (ii) a global modulation of the overall prediction-error magnitude that influences gain. The Cutrell & Marrocco dissociation is therefore robust across the softmax-vs-prediction-error divide that distinguishes the recurrent ViT and PRISM v1. This robustness is itself a methodological asset: a behavioral signature that holds across multiple architectural choices is more likely to reflect a deep computational property of priority-map mechanisms than an idiosyncrasy of any one implementation.

**Implications for evaluating the multi-hub multi-objective system.** If the user's [multi-hub-multi-objective-system](research_db/concepts/multi_hub_multi_objective_system.md) architecture is correct, perturbing the contribution of one hub (say, the MSI hub) to the central self-attention map should produce the same dual-component signature: a spatial bias toward whatever locations that hub is amplifying, plus a global gain modulation reflecting the redistribution of softmax mass. Replicating Cutrell & Marrocco's parietal-microstimulation pattern *across multiple hubs* — and showing that the orienting-vs-alerting balance differs by hub — would be strong evidence that the multi-hub architecture decomposes attention into the kind of substrate that the Posner tripartite framework describes.

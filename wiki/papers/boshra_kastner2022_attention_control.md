# Attention control in the primate brain

## 1. Abstract

Attention is fundamental to all cognition. In the primate brain, it is implemented by a large-scale network that consists of areas spanning all major lobes, also including subcortical regions. Classical attention accounts assume that control over the selection process in this network is exerted by "top-down" mechanisms in the fronto-parietal cortex that influence sensory representations via feedback signals. More recent studies have expanded this view of attentional control. The review starts from a traditional top-down account of attention control and then introduces three more recent control accounts along with supporting evidence: advances in feature-based control (with newly identified prefrontal nodes such as the ventral prearcuate gyrus and temporal nodes such as PITd/fSTS), thalamic control (with pulvinar emerging as a hub that coordinates large-scale cortical interactions), and the control of temporal network dynamics (with theta-rhythmic "sampling vs. shifting" states that alternate at ~4–6 Hz). Boshra and Kastner outline how these emerging accounts can be reconciled and integrated into a unified theory in which attention control is distributed across multiple specialised network nodes whose interactions are temporally orchestrated through the thalamus.

The review is structured around four substantive sections — "Fronto-parietal control of the selection process," "New perspectives on feature-based control," "Coordination of large-scale networks: Thalamic control," and "Attention and the dimension of time: Control of temporal dynamics" — followed by a "Conclusions: Attentional control 2.0" synthesis. The treatment is consistently network-level: the unit of analysis is the interaction between cortical and thalamic nodes rather than the activity of any single neuron or area.

## 2. Why this matters for us

Boshra & Kastner 2022 is the cleanest current statement of the Kastner-lab program: attention control is *not* a purely top-down, fronto-parietal-only affair, but a distributed, large-scale loop in which the pulvinar plays the role of a coordinating hub and the network's behaviour unfolds rhythmically over time. This view of attention as a *thalamically-coordinated, temporally-dynamic cortical network* is the empirical anchor for two of the user's architectural commitments. First, the multi-compartmental, bidirectionally-connected memory stack (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §3) is the computational analog of the fronto-parietal-pulvinar attention loop. Second, the Feedback Transformer (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §1) is the computational analog of the central self-attention substrate that the pulvinar is hypothesised to coordinate. The review's identification of pulvinar as a "time-keeper" / "enabler" that gates inter-areal communication is the biological anchor for treating the Recurrent ViT's recurrent self-attention as more than a sequence-modelling convenience: it is the substrate over which competing top-down and bottom-up signals are arbitrated.

The Kastner lab is one of the most prominent attention-control labs in primate neuroscience, and this review is the most current synthesis of their program. As a recent (2022) review by senior figures in the field, it is the single best citation for any claim in the user's follow-up work about the *current* state of attention-control neuroscience — superseding earlier reviews (Corbetta & Shulman 2002; Kastner & Ungerleider 2000; Posner & Petersen 1990) on coverage of pulvinar coordination and theta dynamics.

## 3. Key claims

The review's central thesis is that attention control should be reconceived as distributed across multiple specialised cortical and subcortical nodes, coordinated by the pulvinar, and unfolding rhythmically over time on a theta cycle. The nine specific claims that comprise this thesis are listed below.

1. The primate attention network spans all major cortical lobes and subcortical structures (thalamus, basal ganglia, superior colliculus). Lesions to many of these nodes — most famously the inferior parietal lobule and superior temporal cortex, but also basal ganglia and thalamus — can produce visuospatial hemineglect, demonstrating that control function is distributed rather than localised to one area.
2. The traditional fronto-parietal top-down model (FEF, dlPFC, LIP, VIP) is broadly correct as a starting point: imaging shows sustained fronto-parietal activity during anticipatory attention, FEF microstimulation produces V4 attention-like enhancement, and PFC lesions attenuate attentional modulation downstream. The fronto-parietal network is "domain-general."
3. **Feature-based control is partly decoupled from spatial control.** The ventral prearcuate gyrus (VPA), lateral to the principal sulcus and anterior to the arcuate sulcus, exhibits sustained feature-selective responses with earlier latencies than FEF, and VPA inactivation abolishes feature-based (but not space-based) attention effects in FEF. VPA therefore acts as a feature-attention source whose signals propagate to FEF and to visual cortex.
4. **A temporal-cortex node also exerts control.** Dorsal posterior inferotemporal cortex (PITd) / floor of STS (fSTS) is strongly connected with VPA, FEF, and LIP, and shows strong attention modulation; PITd microstimulation causally shifts attention allocation. Its neurons respond across feature categories and to objects, suggesting a role in object-based attention. SC inactivation reduces (but does not abolish) attentional modulation in fSTS, implicating an SC → pulvinar → PITd/fSTS pathway.
5. **Pulvinar is a coordinating hub.** Pulvinar is interconnected with cortex via two pathways that parallel the visual hierarchy: a transthalamic feedforward route (L5 → pulvinar → L4 of upstream area) and a feedback route (L6 → pulvinar). It indirectly connects pairs of cortical areas that share a direct corticocortical projection. Simultaneous recordings show that, during the cue-target interval, pulvinar drives synchronised low-frequency (alpha / low-beta, <20 Hz) activity across cortical pairs, with pulvinar → cortex influence dominating cortex → pulvinar influence.
6. **Pulvinar inactivation breaks cortico-cortical coordination.** Inactivating pulvinar weakens functional interactions between cortical areas and attenuates visual-cortex responsiveness — i.e., without pulvinar, cortico-cortical information transmission is compromised. Pulvinar acts both as a "time-keeper" (coordinating inter-areal timing) and as an "enabler" (gating cortical excitability).
7. **Attention is intrinsically rhythmic.** Behaviour fluctuates at ~4–6 Hz (theta) between better- and worse-performance epochs in both humans and monkeys. Neuronal theta phase in fronto-parietal cortex and thalamus is predictive of trial-by-trial detection performance, linking behavioural rhythms to network-level signals.
8. **Two rhythmic states: "sampling" vs. "shifting."** The sampling state (better performance) is characterised by gamma synchrony (>35 Hz) on visual-neuron spikes and beta synchrony (~15–30 Hz) on oculomotor-neuron spikes, with pulvinar coordinating cortico-cortical gamma. The shifting state (worse performance) is characterised by reduced motor-suppressive beta in LIP/FEF and increased parietal-pulvinar alpha synchrony, hypothesised to temporarily interrupt transthalamic gating and create windows for reallocating attention.
9. **Unified theory.** Attention control is distributed across multiple network nodes with specific functions (space, feature, object, time), but is integrated into a single network whose interactions are coordinated through pulvinar and unfold rhythmically. The space-, feature-, and object-based control routes are not mutually exclusive; they synergise.

10. **"Attentional control 2.0."** The traditional fronto-parietal top-down model is correct but incomplete. The updated framework — Attentional control 2.0 — adds (a) parallel non-fronto-parietal feature/object control nodes (VPA, PITd/fSTS); (b) pulvinar as a coordinating hub that gates inter-areal communication; and (c) theta-rhythmic temporal organisation that alternates network states between sampling and shifting regimes. The three additions are treated as complementary refinements rather than replacements of the original model.

## 4. Methods

A narrative review with no new empirical data. The authors synthesise their own and others' results from primate intracranial electrophysiology — single-unit, LFP, microstimulation, pharmacological inactivation — combined with human and monkey neuroimaging. The review is organised into four substantive sections: (i) the traditional fronto-parietal top-down model; (ii) new feature- and object-based control nodes (VPA, PITd/fSTS) and their evidence from microstimulation, inactivation, and selective recording; (iii) thalamic (pulvinar) control as a coordinating hub, with evidence from simultaneous multi-area recordings of cortico-pulvinar functional interactions and from pulvinar inactivation studies; and (iv) rhythmic temporal dynamics, with evidence from theta-phase / behaviour coupling and from sampling-vs-shifting state characterisation. Key cited paradigms include cue-target spatial attention with cue-target intervals, feature-based attention with VPA inactivation, motion-discrimination attention with SC inactivation, and visual-detection tasks for rhythmic-behaviour and theta-phase analyses.

The review's organising construct is *distributed control via thalamic coordination*. Rather than treat attention as a hierarchy with a single apex, the authors frame attention as a network with several specialised cortical control nodes — FEF and dlPFC for space and saccade, VPA for feature, PITd/fSTS for object/temporal, LIP for spatial priority, SC for orienting — whose interactions are organised in time by the pulvinar. The conceptual move is to elevate the pulvinar from a passive relay (its first-order-thalamus characterisation) to an active coordinator (its higher-order-thalamus characterisation, in Sherman's terms), and to use that coordinator to explain how a distributed control network can act coherently on millisecond timescales. The review uses the term "Attentional control 2.0" in its concluding section to mark this departure from the canonical fronto-parietal-only "1.0" model.

## 5. Results

The synthesis is qualitative; the specific quantitative anchors the review provides are:

- **Fronto-parietal areas in NHP:** FEF, dorsolateral and ventrolateral PFC, LIP, V6/V6A, VIP are the conserved attention-network nodes shared with humans. Topographic maps in these areas preferentially represent the contralateral visual hemifield; attention to the contralateral hemifield evokes stronger biasing signals than to the ipsilateral hemifield, supporting an interhemispheric-competition account.
- **FEF microstimulation** at sub-threshold currents enhances discriminability in downstream areas (notably V4) and is causally sufficient to produce attention-like behavioural effects. Human TMS over FEF produces qualitatively similar effects, corroborating cross-species generality.
- **PFC lesions** attenuate attentional modulation in visual cortex, supplying causal evidence for fronto-parietal feedback being a *source* (not just a correlate) of extrastriate attentional gain.
- **VPA inactivation** selectively abolishes feature-based but not space-based attention effects in FEF, and impairs feature-based attention task performance. VPA feature-selective responses have earlier latencies than FEF, consistent with VPA → FEF being a feedforward (rather than feedback) flow of feature information.
- **PITd microstimulation** causally shifts the attended location. PITd lesions / SC inactivation produce hemineglect-like deficits in motion-discrimination attention tasks. PITd cells respond across feature categories and to objects without exhibiting strong intrinsic feature selectivity, consistent with PITd acting as a domain-general control node operating over feature representations elsewhere.
- **Pulvinar–cortex anatomy.** Pulvinar projects to and receives projections from essentially the entire cortical visual system. Two pathways are emphasised: a transthalamic feedforward route (L5 cortex → pulvinar → L4 of higher area, paralleling a direct corticocortical projection) and a corticothalamic feedback route (L6 cortex → pulvinar). Pulvinar's expansion across primate evolution scales with that of neocortex, consistent with a role in coordinating the expanded cortical mantle.
- **Pulvinar coordination of cortico-cortical synchrony** operates in a low-frequency band (alpha / low-beta, <20 Hz) during the cue-target interval. Simultaneous recordings from two directly-interconnected cortical areas plus their pulvinar projection zone show that pulvinar → cortex Granger-style influence dominates cortex → pulvinar influence on this coordination.
- **Pulvinar inactivation** weakens cortico-cortical functional interactions and greatly attenuates visual-cortex responsiveness, demonstrating that pulvinar is causally necessary for inter-areal communication and for cortical excitability.
- **Behavioural attention rhythms** are at ~4–6 Hz (theta), conserved between humans and monkeys. The phase of the neuronal theta rhythm in fronto-parietal cortex and thalamus relative to target appearance predicts behavioural outcome on a trial-by-trial basis.
- **Sampling state:** gamma (>35 Hz) on visual-neuron spikes; beta (~15–30 Hz) on oculomotor-neuron spikes; pulvinar coordinates inter-areal gamma. Associated with better detection performance.
- **Shifting state:** decreased motor-suppressive beta in LIP / FEF; increased parietal-pulvinar alpha synchrony, hypothesised to temporarily interrupt the transthalamic gating function and create opportunities for re-allocating attention. Associated with relatively worse detection performance but greater behavioural flexibility.

The review does not report novel single numbers; its contribution is a synthesis of these qualitative signatures into a unified network-level theory. Quantitative claims requiring source-level inspection (effect sizes of FEF microstimulation, Granger directionality magnitudes, theta-phase prediction strengths) should be traced through the primary references cited in §8.

## 6. Critique / limitations

The review is a position piece: a deliberately schematic synthesis aimed at updating the canonical fronto-parietal model. As such, it has the strengths and weaknesses of a Current Opinion piece. The strengths are clarity, scope, and an explicit synthesis of three lines of work that are usually treated separately (feature control via VPA / PITd, thalamic control via pulvinar, temporal control via theta rhythms). The weaknesses are that the synthesis is hypothesis-rich and mechanism-light: the proposed "sampling vs shifting" framework, while empirically supported by theta-phase / behaviour correlations and by gamma/beta/alpha state characterisations, is not given a circuit-level mechanistic implementation. The review identifies pulvinar as a "time-keeper" / "enabler" but does not commit to which pulvinar cells (driver-projecting L5-input cells vs modulator-projecting L6-input cells; cf. sherman2022_ctc_loop) implement which function. The PITd / fSTS literature is also young, and the relationship between PITd and fSTS (overlapping? adjacent? functionally distinct?) is acknowledged as unresolved.

The functional taxonomy of fronto-parietal areas is also less crisp than the review's diagram suggests. FEF, dlPFC, VPA, LIP, VIP, and V6/V6A all show overlapping signatures of attentional modulation, and the review's mapping of "FEF for space, VPA for feature, PITd for object" is more a useful starting framework than a settled functional dissection. The same cells that exhibit feature selectivity often exhibit spatial selectivity, and the segregation between space-, feature-, and object-attention may be a matter of population-level emphasis rather than a strict per-cell partitioning. The review is candid that VPA "exhibits sustained feature-selective responses that are obtained at earlier response latencies than in FEF," suggesting a feedforward feature-information flow VPA → FEF — but does not engage with whether VPA itself derives its feature selectivity from ventral-stream areas (V4, IT) or computes it locally.

A subtler concern is that the review largely treats the *control* problem as identifying which areas carry control signals and how those signals are coordinated, rather than addressing the harder question of how control signals are *learned*. The relationship between the fronto-parietal-pulvinar control network and reinforcement-learning / motivational signals from basal ganglia and dopamine is explicitly placed out of scope ("control mechanisms related to why an item is selected... are beyond the scope of this review"). For computational architectures, this is a substantive gap: the review supplies the *substrate* over which attention is allocated but says little about the optimisation pressure that shapes attention allocation.

The review is also predominantly a primate-electrophysiology synthesis. The closed-loop mouse-V1 literature (Keller, Bonhoeffer & Hübener 2012; Attinger et al. 2017) and the rodent-thalamus literature (Halassa lab work on the TRN; Wimmer et al. on PFC-MD loops) are not engaged in depth, and the cross-species generalisation of the pulvinar's role to rodent higher-order thalamus is not adjudicated. The cross-species evidence from sherman2022_ctc_loop (mouse) and mckinnon_mo_sherman2025_transthalamic_v1 (mouse) is *consistent* with the primate picture the review paints, but the homology is not formally argued by either side.

A connected concern is the relationship between the pulvinar-as-coordinator account and the reticular-thalamic-nucleus (TRN) literature. TRN-mediated thalamic gating is a major rodent-attention finding (Wimmer et al.; Halassa lab) but is not engaged here. Whether the pulvinar-coordination signature the Kastner lab documents in primate is mediated by TRN gating, by intrinsic pulvinar circuitry, or by L6 cortico-thalamic feedback is left open. From a computational-architecture standpoint this matters because the three possibilities motivate different connectivity patterns: TRN gating would suggest a separate gating module between the central self-attention substrate and the cortical hubs; intrinsic pulvinar circuitry would suggest the central self-attention substrate itself should be capable of self-modulating its gating; and L6 cortico-thalamic feedback would push the gating into the descending projections from each hub.

Finally, the review proposes that the sampling and shifting states alternate at a theta rate (~4–6 Hz), but the mechanism by which the brain *transitions* between the two states — what triggers a shift, what resets the rhythm, what selects the new attended location — is left open. This is consistent with the review being a Current Opinion piece rather than a primary research article, but a reader looking for a generative model of the dynamics rather than a catalogue of empirical signatures will find the review descriptive.

A final concern is that the proposed integration of the three "Attentional control 2.0" lines (feature, thalamic, temporal) into a single network is more an organising aspiration than a derived consequence. The three lines were independently developed, are anchored in mostly non-overlapping experimental paradigms, and the review does not provide a single experiment that simultaneously probes feature control via VPA, thalamic coordination via pulvinar, and theta-rhythmic state alternations. The unified theory the review proposes is thus a productive hypothesis to test rather than an already-established framework. The user's program, in implementing all three in a single computational architecture (the Feedback Transformer + GridCell RNN + multi-compartmental memory stack), can be read as a direct computational test of whether the unified theory can in fact be made to work as a single coherent system.

## 7. Connection to our work

This paper is load-bearing for the user's architectural program on at least six interlocking points. The Kastner lab is one of the most prominent attention-control labs in primate neuroscience, and the review's "Attentional control 2.0" framing — pulvinar as hub, theta as orchestrator, multiple control nodes operating in tandem — directly motivates several of the user's architectural commitments. The detailed mapping is below.

**(i) Pulvinar as central attention substrate maps onto the Recurrent ViT's central self-attention module.** The review's most distinctive empirical claim is that pulvinar — not any cortical area — drives coordinated cortico-cortical interactions during attention, and that inactivating pulvinar breaks cortico-cortical communication. The Kastner lab's pulvinar-as-hub framing is the biological anchor for treating the Recurrent ViT's central self-attention layer as the substrate where multiple feedback streams (bottom-up sensory, top-down memory, lateral parallel) are arbitrated. The published Recurrent ViT (2502.10955) implements a single instance of this with one feedback source $H^{(t-1)}$; the Feedback Transformer (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §1) generalises it to many sources, exactly paralleling the pulvinar's role as a hub through which "control signals route through efficiently, either as a means to coordinate processing between control areas or to propagate from control nodes to sensory areas." The competition for attention control between hubs (`competition_emergent_predictive_coding`) is the computational analog of the competition between fronto-parietal feedback, VPA feature-feedback, PITd/fSTS object-feedback, and SC-pulvinar orienting signals that the review describes as the attention network's joint operating regime.

Importantly, the review's specific causal claim — that pulvinar inactivation *attenuates visual-cortex responsiveness* in addition to reducing cortico-cortical synchrony — implies that the central self-attention substrate in the user's architecture should not be a passive coordination layer but should actively gate the gain of upstream sensory processing. The Feedback Transformer's Hadamard-product Q/K projections supply exactly this kind of multiplicative gating; the alternative architecture in which feedback is purely additive (closer to a relay) would not match the pulvinar-as-enabler phenomenology.

**(ii) Cortico-thalamo-cortical loops as the user's signature concept.** The user's program elevates `cortico-thalamo-cortical-loops` to a first-class organising concept (TAXONOMY.md §"Additional concepts from the notes' cite trail"). The review provides the modern primate-attention case for that concept: cortex-pulvinar-cortex loops are *the* mechanism by which the brain organises a large-scale attention network in time. Together with sherman2022_ctc_loop (anatomy and driver/modulator physiology of the CTC loop in mouse), sherman_guillery2011_distinct_functions (the original driver/modulator distinction), and mckinnon_mo_sherman2025_transthalamic_v1 (a recent demonstration of transthalamic V1 modulation), this paper anchors the primate-attention side of the user's CTC concept. PRISM v2's slow / fast memory split, with the slow stream playing a coordinating modulatory role analogous to pulvinar, is the architectural commitment most directly supported.

The two transthalamic pathways the review invokes — feedforward (L5 → pulvinar → L4 of higher area) and feedback (L6 → pulvinar) — map naturally onto the user's descending- and ascending-projection scheme (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §3). The L5-driver feedforward route, which "indirectly connects two cortical areas that share a direct cortico-cortical projection," is the biological motivation for treating the transthalamic detour not as a noisy parallel copy of cortico-cortical projections but as the primary coordinating substrate, with direct cortico-cortical projections supplying the local lateral context. This is precisely the architectural division the user's program makes: the Feedback Transformer is the global coordinating substrate (pulvinar analog) and the GridCell RNN's within-grid attention is the local lateral context (intra-cortical analog).

**(iii) Multiple feature- and object-attention control nodes support multi-hub-multi-objective architecture.** The review's identification of VPA (feature), PITd/fSTS (object/temporal), FEF (space/saccade), and LIP (space/priority) as functionally distinct control nodes with distinct projection profiles is precisely the architectural picture the `multi_hub_multi_objective_system` concept describes: many objective-specific hubs, each with its own memory states, all feeding back into a central self-attention module. The pulvinar's role of coordinating these hubs maps onto the central Feedback Transformer; each cortical hub maps onto a GridCell RNN whose state contributes to the Q/K projections via Hadamard broadcasting. The review does not anticipate this computational mapping but supplies the empirical structure on which it rests.

In particular, the review's finding that VPA → FEF feature-information flow has *earlier latencies than FEF's own attention modulation* — i.e., feature-information leads spatial control — is the biological analog of the user's design choice to let each hub maintain its own memory states that feed into the central attention substrate, rather than serialising the control flow through a single apex hub. A purely fronto-parietal model would predict that all control signals converge in FEF / dlPFC before reaching visual cortex; the Kastner-lab picture instead has multiple control nodes contributing in parallel, with the pulvinar coordinating their joint effect. The user's parallel-hub architecture is the computational realisation of the parallel-control-node empirics.

**(iv) Temporal dynamics — theta sampling/shifting — support recurrent computation.** The review's strongest empirical contribution is that attention is intrinsically rhythmic, with ~4–6 Hz alternations between "sampling" (enhanced sensory processing) and "shifting" (reallocation opportunity) states. This is direct neurophysiological support for the central architectural commitment of the user's iterative variational encoder-decoder (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §4): forward-reasoning passes ($n_{FR}$) are sampling-like (gather and refine information about the current attended content); the transition from forward to backward reasoning, and the iterative reconstruction proposals across $n_{BR}$ steps, are shifting-like (reallocate computation to a new latent / reconstruction hypothesis). The Recurrent ViT paper's qualitative finding (`the_user_architectural_program.md` §6) that attention maps "focus, defocus, and reactivate" over recurrent steps mirrors the sampling/shifting alternation. The user's architecture is, in this framing, a computational instantiation of the theta-rhythmic attention regime the Kastner lab characterises empirically.

The specific spectral signatures the review reports — gamma synchrony on visual-neuron spikes, beta on oculomotor spikes, alpha on parietal-pulvinar shifts — also speak to the choice of recurrent timescale in the architecture. PRISM v2's slow-fast memory split (`PRISM_V2_PROPOSAL.md` §3.3) is the most direct translation: the fast memory (V1-level, fine spatial grid) handles within-sample integration on a gamma-like fast timescale, while the slow memory (deeper, coarser grid) handles cross-sample re-arbitration on an alpha-like slow timescale, with the central self-attention substrate playing the pulvinar-like role of coordinating the two. This is a falsifiable architectural commitment: a model with only one recurrent timescale should under-perform a model with two timescales matched to the empirical gamma/alpha dichotomy.

**(v) Theta-rhythmic control of the inter-hub attention competition.** The user's `competition_emergent_predictive_coding` thesis predicts that hubs compete for control of the central self-attention map, with each hub trying to predict not only the sensory input but also its competitors' attention contributions. The review's sampling-vs-shifting account adds a temporal structure to this competition: during sampling states the currently-winning hub configuration is locked in (gamma synchrony, transthalamic gating enabled), and during shifting states the lock is released (decreased motor-suppressive beta, parietal-pulvinar alpha synchrony interrupts gating), creating an opportunity for a different hub configuration to take over. This is the biological analog of an architectural choice the user has not yet fully specified: under what schedule should hubs re-arbitrate their contributions to the central self-attention map? The Kastner-lab answer is "rhythmically, on a theta cycle, with the rhythm coordinated by the central self-attention substrate itself." Translating this into the user's architecture would amount to imposing a theta-like alternation between consolidation passes (forward reasoning over a stable internal state) and re-arbitration passes (an explicit step in which hubs renegotiate their Q/K contributions). The Recurrent ViT's iterative steps already implement something like this implicitly; making the schedule explicit would be a concrete architectural follow-up.

A further point of leverage is the review's observation that the shifting state is associated with *relatively worse* immediate detection performance but greater behavioural flexibility. In the user's architecture, this corresponds to a trade-off between exploitation (forward-reasoning passes that refine a stable interpretation) and exploration (re-arbitration steps that may upend the current interpretation). The Kastner-lab data suggest that this trade-off is not resolved once and for all but is cycled through continuously — the brain does not settle into one mode and stay there. This argues against architectures in which the iterative refinement converges to a fixed point and stays there, and in favour of architectures in which the iteration trajectory is intrinsically non-stationary, with periodic re-shuffling of the dominant hub configuration. The user's iterative VAE, with its $n_{FR}$ forward then $n_{BR}$ backward passes, already has this character; the review supplies the empirical justification for keeping it that way rather than collapsing it to a single converged-fixed-point pass.

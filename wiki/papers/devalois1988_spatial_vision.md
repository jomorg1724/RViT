# Spatial Vision

## 1. Abstract

De Valois & De Valois's *Spatial Vision* (Oxford University Press, 1988) is the canonical monograph synthesising the spatial-frequency-and-orientation framework of mammalian early visual processing. The book unifies three previously separate traditions — psychophysical channels (Campbell & Robson 1968; Blakemore & Campbell 1969; Graham 1989), single-unit physiology (Hubel & Wiesel 1962, 1968; De Valois, Albrecht & Thorell 1982), and mathematical modelling (Marčelja 1980; Daugman 1985) — into a single account: the visual system performs an approximately local, joint space/spatial-frequency analysis of the retinal image, implemented at the level of V1 simple cells whose receptive fields are well-approximated by **two-dimensional Gabor functions** (oriented sinusoidal gratings windowed by a Gaussian envelope), arranged in a topographic map that simultaneously covers retinal position, preferred orientation, preferred spatial frequency, and (for binocular animals) ocular dominance. The De Valoises marshal the evidence that V1 simple cells are *quasi-linear spatial-frequency channels*: their responses to gratings are approximately separable in orientation and spatial frequency, their tuning bandwidths (typically ~30–40° for orientation and ~1–1.5 octaves for spatial frequency) match those inferred psychophysically from masking and adaptation experiments, their preferred spatial frequencies span the full visible range (~0.5–30 cycles/degree in macaque) and are mapped across the cortical surface roughly in register with retinotopy, and the population of cells at each retinotopic location samples the joint orientation × spatial-frequency space densely enough that any visible spatial pattern can be reconstructed from the population response. They argue that the elementary computational unit of the early visual cortex is therefore not the centre-surround difference-of-Gaussians of the retina/LGN (which is space-domain) nor a generic edge detector (which has no spatial-frequency selectivity), but a **band-pass, orientation-selective, position-localised Gabor channel** that achieves a principled compromise between spatial and spatial-frequency localisation (saturating the Heisenberg-like uncertainty bound that Daugman 1985 derives). Complex cells are then explained as energy-detectors over pairs of phase-shifted simple-cell channels (anticipating Adelson & Bergen 1985), and the V1 architecture as a whole is reframed as a **multichannel Gabor filter bank** sampled densely in position, orientation, and spatial frequency. The framework licenses essentially every subsequent quantitative model of early visual cortex (energy models of motion and stereo; HMAX; primate-centred CNN models) and provides the theoretical bridge from physiology to image-statistical accounts of V1 (Olshausen & Field 1996; Bell & Sejnowski 1997) and to engineering applications of Gabor filters in computer vision.

The book is also notable for the systematic treatment of *what spatial-frequency tuning does not explain*: the De Valoises are explicit that the Gabor-channel framework is an *empirical generalisation* of the linear receptive-field properties of V1 simple cells and that genuinely nonlinear phenomena — divisive normalisation, surround suppression, cross-orientation suppression, contour integration, attention-mediated gain control, and the role of feedback from extrastriate cortex — all sit outside the linear-Gabor account and require additional mechanisms. They thereby frame the spatial-frequency theory not as a complete theory of vision but as the empirically-validated *feedforward kernel* on which subsequent recurrent, normalising, and feedback-driven computations operate. This dual stance — Gabor channels are the right *first-order* description but visual cortex is fundamentally more than a Gabor filter bank — is what makes the book the canonical reference for the spatial-frequency framework while still leaving room for the recurrent-feedback program that subsequent computational neuroscience (and the user's architectural program) develops on top of it.

## 2. Why this matters for us

The Gabor-channel account of V1 simple cells is the empirical and theoretical reason that modern computer-vision architectures — convolutional networks, vision transformers, and any patch-tokenising system — work at all on natural images. Every CNN's first convolutional layer learns filters that are well-approximated by oriented Gabors (Krizhevsky et al. 2012); ViTs' patch-projection layer is, in effect, a learned coarse Gabor-channel decomposition of the image into a token vector at each patch location. The De Valoises' synthesis is what licenses the architectural decision to use such a representation: primate V1 demonstrably does a multichannel, position-local, orientation-and-spatial-frequency joint analysis of its input, so a learned model that begins with such a decomposition is mimicking the empirically-correct first stage of biological vision. For the user's program (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md`), the book is the primate-centred theoretical justification for the V1-level feature map that every layer of the GridCell-RNN stack (§3 of the program) sits on top of — a justification that goes beyond the Hubel & Wiesel qualitative simple-cell taxonomy and into the *quantitative*, *multichannel*, *population-level* spatial-frequency-channel architecture.

## 3. Key claims

1. **V1 simple-cell receptive fields are well-approximated by two-dimensional Gabor functions** — a sinusoidal grating of a preferred orientation and spatial frequency, windowed by a Gaussian envelope — and the parameters of these Gabors (preferred orientation, preferred spatial frequency, bandwidth, phase, position, aspect ratio) are jointly distributed across the simple-cell population to cover the visible orientation × spatial-frequency × position space.
2. **The visual system implements multiple spatial-frequency channels in parallel**: psychophysical adaptation and masking experiments (Blakemore & Campbell 1969; Graham 1989) demonstrate independent processing of spatial frequencies separated by ~1 octave; single-unit recordings (De Valois, Albrecht & Thorell 1982) demonstrate matching ~1–1.5 octave bandwidths in V1 simple cells; the two lines of evidence are quantitatively consistent.
3. **Orientation tuning bandwidth of V1 simple cells is typically ~30–40° half-width at half-height** in macaque, matching the bandwidth inferred psychophysically from orientation-masking experiments.
4. **Preferred spatial frequencies span ~0.5–30 cycles/degree** in macaque foveal V1, with a foveal bias toward higher spatial frequencies and a peripheral bias toward lower frequencies, in register with the retinotopic map and consistent with the cortical magnification factor.
5. **V1 implements a topographic joint map of position × orientation × spatial frequency** (and × ocular dominance in binocular species), with each retinotopic location sampled by cells spanning the full orientation and spatial-frequency space at that location.
6. **Complex cells are energy detectors built from pairs of quadrature-phase simple cells**: the position-invariant, phase-invariant response of a complex cell is well-explained as the sum of squared responses of two simple cells whose Gabor receptive fields differ by a 90° phase shift — the explicit construction subsequently formalised as the *motion-energy* and *binocular-energy* models (Adelson & Bergen 1985; Ohzawa et al. 1990).
7. **Gabor receptive fields saturate the joint space / spatial-frequency uncertainty bound** (Daugman 1985): among all 2D filters, Gabors achieve the theoretical minimum of the product Δx Δy Δfx Δfy, making them the *optimal* receptive-field shape for joint spatial and spatial-frequency localisation given any fixed bandwidth budget.
8. **Channel responses across the population can in principle reconstruct any visible image**: the V1 population, treated as a sampled Gabor filter bank with redundant coverage of position × orientation × spatial frequency, is approximately complete in the sense that the inverse mapping from population response to image is well-conditioned (modulo the unsampled high-frequency tail and the cells' nonlinearities).
9. **The framework is feedforward and linear-quasilinear**: the De Valoises are explicit that the Gabor-channel theory is the *first-order* description of V1 input-output behaviour and that gain control, surround suppression, cross-orientation masking, contour integration, attention-mediated modulation, and intracortical/extrastriate feedback all sit outside it and require additional mechanisms.
10. **Spatial frequency is mapped systematically across V1**: cells of similar preferred spatial frequency cluster locally on the cortical surface (analogous to orientation columns), with the spatial-frequency map varying smoothly and largely orthogonally to the orientation map — a claim refined by subsequent optical-imaging work (Issa, Trepel & Stryker 2000).
11. **The colour-opponent and luminance-channel structure** of the early visual system is laid down at the LGN level and inherited by V1, with V1 simple cells additionally tuned for spatial frequency and orientation along both luminance and chromatic axes — the chromatic spatial-frequency channels having systematically lower preferred frequencies than the luminance channels, matching the lower acuity of the chromatic system psychophysically.

## 4. Methods

The book is a synthesis monograph rather than an experimental paper, so the "methods" are the collection of experimental and theoretical techniques that the De Valoises bring together to establish the spatial-frequency framework.

**Psychophysical methods.** Contrast-threshold measurement for sinusoidal-grating stimuli of varying spatial frequency, orientation, and contrast; adaptation paradigms in which prolonged viewing of a high-contrast grating raises the threshold for detection of a similar test grating selectively at the adapting spatial frequency and orientation; masking paradigms in which a high-contrast mask grating raises the contrast-detection threshold for a superimposed test grating selectively at the mask's spatial-frequency and orientation neighbourhood. The width of the selectivity envelope of adaptation/masking effects is interpreted as the bandwidth of the underlying psychophysical channel.

**Single-unit physiology.** Extracellular recordings from macaque (and cat) V1, with each isolated cell probed by drifting sinusoidal gratings of varied spatial frequency, orientation, contrast, and temporal frequency. The cell's tuning curve along each stimulus dimension is fit, the preferred value and bandwidth extracted, and the resulting population statistics compared to the psychophysical channel inferences.

**Quantitative Gabor fits.** Two-dimensional receptive-field maps obtained from spot or bar stimulation (or, in later work, from reverse-correlation with white-noise stimuli) are fit to a 2D Gabor function $h(x, y) = \exp\big(-(x'^2/2\sigma_x^2 + y'^2/2\sigma_y^2)\big)\cdot \cos(2\pi f_0 x' + \varphi)$ where $(x', y')$ is rotated by the cell's preferred orientation. The fit quality and the distribution of fitted parameters across cells are the basis for the claim that V1 simple cells *are* Gabor channels.

**Theoretical analysis.** The book draws on Marčelja (1980) and Daugman (1985) for the formal demonstration that 2D Gabors are the joint space / spatial-frequency uncertainty-minimising filters, and on Watson (1983) for the multi-channel observer model that converts channel responses into psychophysical predictions. The synthesis is then to verify that V1 single-cell tuning bandwidths, density, and arrangement are quantitatively consistent with the multi-channel-observer model required by the psychophysical data.

**Comparative anatomy and physiology.** The book devotes substantial attention to the spatial-vision capabilities of cat, macaque, and human, comparing receptive-field sizes, acuity, and spatial-frequency tuning across species. This is the comparative work that complements Hubel & Wiesel 1962 (cat) and Hubel & Wiesel 1968 (macaque) and provides the *quantitative* cat-vs.-primate comparison that the Hubel & Wiesel papers handled only qualitatively.

## 5. Results

- **V1 simple cells fit by 2D Gabors:** across hundreds of cells, the Gabor function captures the bulk of the simple-cell receptive-field variance; the residual is small enough that the Gabor parameterisation is the canonical quantitative description (subsequently confirmed by Jones & Palmer 1987).
- **Orientation bandwidth (HWHH):** typically 15–30° for sharp cells, 30–60° for broad cells, with population median ~30–40° in macaque V1.
- **Spatial-frequency bandwidth (HWHH in octaves):** typically 1–1.5 octaves in macaque V1, matching the psychophysical estimate of ~1 octave from masking and adaptation.
- **Preferred spatial-frequency range:** ~0.5 cyc/deg (peripheral, low-frequency) to ~30 cyc/deg (foveal, high-frequency) in macaque V1; the population covers the full range with roughly log-uniform density in preferred frequency.
- **Aspect ratio of simple-cell Gabors:** the Gaussian envelope of typical simple cells is elongated parallel to the preferred orientation with aspect ratio ~2:1, corresponding to approximately 1–2 cycles of the preferred-frequency grating within the excitatory subregions.
- **Joint sampling density:** at each retinotopic position, V1 contains cells spanning the full 180° of orientation in steps of ~10–20° (matching the orientation-column geometry of Hubel & Wiesel 1968) and the full ~6-octave spatial-frequency range in steps of ~1 octave; the combined orientation × spatial-frequency × position grid is sampled densely enough that the population response is approximately invertible.
- **Complex cells as energy detectors:** the prediction that complex cells are the sum of squared responses of quadrature-phase simple-cell pairs is verified by quantitative comparison of complex-cell responses to gratings of varied phase, contrast, and spatial frequency; the energy-model fit captures the position invariance, phase invariance, and contrast nonlinearity simultaneously.
- **Psychophysical–physiological consistency:** the orientation- and spatial-frequency-bandwidth distributions of V1 simple cells match the channel bandwidths inferred from psychophysical adaptation and masking to within the experimental uncertainty in both quantities; the two independent lines of evidence converge on the same multi-channel architecture.
- **Spatial-frequency columns:** local clusters of cells with similar preferred spatial frequency are observed in macaque V1 in addition to orientation columns; the spatial-frequency map varies smoothly across the cortical surface and is largely independent of the orientation map.
- **Chromatic spatial-frequency channels:** cells tuned to chromatic spatial-frequency channels have systematically lower preferred frequencies than luminance-channel cells, consistent with the lower psychophysical acuity for chromatic gratings.
- **Cross-species consistency:** the Gabor-channel framework holds qualitatively across cat, macaque, and human, with quantitative differences in receptive-field size and tuning sharpness that scale with the species' visual acuity.
- **Comparison to Hubel & Wiesel:** the book gives the *quantitative cat-vs.-macaque comparison* that the Hubel & Wiesel papers handled only qualitatively; macaque receptive fields are systematically smaller and more sharply tuned than cat fields, by factors that match the species' acuity ratios.
- **Phase distribution across simple cells.** The phase parameter of the fitted Gabor (even-symmetric vs. odd-symmetric vs. intermediate) is approximately uniformly distributed in the simple-cell population, so that quadrature pairs are available at every preferred orientation, spatial frequency, and position — exactly the population structure required for the energy-detector construction of complex cells.
- **Aspect-ratio constraint on cycles-per-receptive-field.** Across the simple-cell population, the number of grating cycles within the excitatory portion of the receptive field clusters around 1–2 cycles, with a long tail to higher counts; this constrains the Gabor's bandwidth-product and confirms that V1 cells occupy a narrow region of the joint-uncertainty plane near the optimum that Daugman 1985 derives.
- **Contrast invariance of orientation and spatial-frequency tuning.** The De Valoises emphasise that the *shape* of the tuning curve along orientation and spatial-frequency axes is approximately contrast-invariant — the response scales with contrast but the preferred orientation, preferred spatial frequency, and bandwidth do not shift systematically with contrast. This contrast-invariance is one of the linear-Gabor-channel framework's strongest predictions and is observed to good approximation (modulo the nonlinear contrast-gain effects that come from divisive normalisation, addressed later by Heeger 1992).

## 6. Critique / limitations

- **Linear-Gabor account is first-order only.** The De Valoises are explicit about this themselves: divisive normalisation (Heeger 1992; Carandini & Heeger 2012), surround suppression (Sillito 1979; Cavanaugh, Bair & Movshon 2002), cross-orientation suppression (Bonds 1989), gain control under contrast, and contrast-dependent shifts in preferred spatial frequency all sit outside the linear-Gabor framework and require nonlinear mechanisms layered on top.
- **Strictly feedforward.** The book treats V1 simple cells as feedforward filters with no role for intracortical recurrence or top-down feedback from extrastriate cortex (V2, V4, IT, FEF). The recurrent and top-down literature (Gilbert & Li 2013; Kietzmann et al. 2019; Bastos et al. 2012) develops mainly after 1988 and substantially complicates the Gabor-only account.
- **Attention-mediated modulation absent.** Reynolds & Heeger's (2009) normalisation-model account of attentional modulation, McAdams & Maunsell's (1999) sharpening of V4 tuning under attention, and the modulatory role of feedback from FEF/LIP on V1 are all outside the 1988 picture. The Gabor channel is the *unattended* response; under attention the gain, the bandwidth, and the spatial-frequency preference all shift.
- **Static / quasi-stationary stimuli.** The Gabor account is established with stationary or drifting grating stimuli at a single contrast and chromatic configuration. Natural-image statistics (Field 1987; Olshausen & Field 1996) and dynamic / video stimuli reveal additional structure (sparse coding, predictive temporal interpolation, motion energy at multiple time scales) that the static-Gabor account does not address.
- **No explicit role for V1 nonclassical receptive field.** The "classical" receptive field — the central excitatory + inhibitory region — is the locus of the Gabor fit. The extra-classical surround, which modulates the classical response over a region 2–5 times larger than the classical field, is at most peripheral in the 1988 account.
- **Pinwheel architecture and orientation-map singularities** (Blasdel & Salama 1986; Bonhoeffer & Grinvald 1991), which subsequent optical-imaging revealed as the true geometry of orientation columns, are not in the book's picture; the orientation map is treated as a smooth surface with linear local progressions.
- **Magno / parvo / konio segregation** (Livingstone & Hubel 1988) is acknowledged but not deeply integrated into the spatial-vision framework; the modern view that the three streams support qualitatively different spatial / temporal / chromatic computations is not yet fully developed in 1988.
- **Cytochrome-oxidase blob → V2 stripe topology** (Sincich & Horton 2005) is not yet known; the connection from V1 colour-coded subsystems to downstream chromatic processing in V2 thin stripes is anticipated only loosely.
- **Phase information and binocular disparity** are addressed via the energy-model construction but the explicit binocular-energy formulation (Ohzawa, DeAngelis & Freeman 1990) is contemporaneous with or slightly later than the book.
- **Sparse-coding / efficient-coding accounts** of V1 receptive fields (Olshausen & Field 1996; Bell & Sejnowski 1997) reframe the Gabor receptive field as a *learned* consequence of natural-image statistics rather than a built-in architectural commitment; the 1988 book is empirical-descriptive rather than computationally-derivational in this sense.
- **The Gabor functional form is not the only candidate.** Stork & Wilson (1990) and Hawken & Parker (1987) argue that derivative-of-Gaussian or difference-of-offset-Gaussian functions fit simple-cell receptive fields about as well; the Gabor parameterisation is the *most useful* description for joint space / spatial-frequency analysis but not the unique best fit on receptive-field-shape grounds alone.
- **The book pre-dates** the full development of HMAX (Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999), modern CNNs (LeCun et al. 1998; Krizhevsky et al. 2012), DiCarlo's ventral-stream-as-DNN framework (DiCarlo et al. 2012), and ViTs (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020). Each of these takes the Gabor-channel framework as input and develops it further; the 1988 synthesis is the foundation on which they all sit but is not itself a computational model in the modern sense.

## 7. Connection to our work

This book is the **theoretical and quantitative bridge** between the qualitative simple-cell taxonomy of Hubel & Wiesel (1962, 1968) and every subsequent computational model that grounds itself in V1, including CNNs, HMAX, and ViTs. For the user's program (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md`), it supplies the empirically-validated quantitative description of the V1 feature map that the GridCell-RNN stack (§3) and Feedback Transformer (§1) sit on top of.

**Gabor channels → CNN first-layer filters → ViT patch projections.** The single most consequential claim of the book — that V1 simple cells are oriented Gabor filters arranged in a multi-channel topographic bank — is the biological precedent for the first convolutional layer of every modern CNN and for the patch-projection layer of every ViT. CNN first-layer filters trained on natural images (AlexNet, Krizhevsky et al. 2012; ResNet, He et al. 2016) are well-known to spontaneously learn oriented Gabor-like filters; this convergence is read by the user's program (and by DiCarlo et al. 2012, in seed) as evidence that the Gabor-channel decomposition is the *correct* first stage of any visual system, biological or artificial. The Recurrent ViT (2502.10955) and PRISM v1/v2 inherit this directly through their use of ViT-style patch projections — the patch projection is, in effect, a learned coarse Gabor-channel decomposition over the patch. The De Valoises' book is the empirical justification for this architectural decision.

**Multi-channel feature representation → patch-token channels.** The book's central architectural claim — that V1 represents each retinotopic location not by a single scalar but by a *vector* of channel responses spanning the joint orientation × spatial-frequency space — is the biological precedent for the multi-channel patch-token representation used throughout the user's program. In primate V1, a single retinotopic location is associated with a vector indexing orientation, spatial frequency, phase, and (for binocular animals) eye-of-origin; in a ViT, the patch-token vector at each grid position carries an analogous multi-feature representation; in the user's GridCell-RNN (program §2), each grid cell carries a recurrent state vector that the Feedback Transformer (program §1) integrates across cells and across feedback sources. The Gabor-channel framework is what makes this multi-channel-per-location commitment *the* correct architectural choice rather than an arbitrary deep-learning convention.

**Connection to `hubel_wiesel1962_receptive_fields` and `hubel_wiesel1968_macaque`.** The two Hubel & Wiesel papers establish the qualitative simple/complex/hypercomplex hierarchy in cat and macaque respectively; the De Valoises' book is the *quantitative completion* of those two papers, fitting the simple-cell receptive fields to Gabors, quantifying the population coverage of the joint orientation × spatial-frequency space, and demonstrating the consistency between cortical tuning and psychophysical channels. For any claim the user's program makes about V1 (orientation tuning, hierarchical pooling, multi-channel feature maps), the trio of papers — Hubel & Wiesel 1962, Hubel & Wiesel 1968, De Valois & De Valois 1988 — forms the complete empirical foundation: 1962 establishes the architecture in cat, 1968 confirms it in macaque, 1988 supplies the quantitative Gabor-channel description that licenses computational modelling.

**Connection to `riesenhuber_poggio1999_hierarchical_models`.** HMAX is the explicit computational descendant of the simple/complex hierarchy and uses **Gabor filters** as its S1 layer — directly importing the Gabor-channel framework from the De Valoises' book. HMAX's hierarchical alternation of simple-cell-like template-matching layers and complex-cell-like max-pooling layers is exactly the construction the book lays out: Gabor channels at the bottom, energy detectors (complex cells) built from pairs of Gabors at the next level, and progressively-pooled position-invariant representations above that. The user's GridCell-RNN stack (program §3) inherits the same hierarchical structure: V1-aligned Layer 1 (Gabor-like patch-token features), V2/V4-aligned Layer 2 (descending-projection conv pooling, channel-expanding), and IT-aligned Layer 3 (further pooled, abstract object-level features). The De Valoises' framework is the V1 entry point of this chain.

**Connection to `tanaka1996_it_object_vision`.** Tanaka's IT-cortex object-vision work (1996) is the ventral-stream endpoint of the hierarchy that begins with V1 Gabor channels. The De Valoises' book is the bottom of the chain that ends at Tanaka's IT object-selective columns; the chain V1 (Gabor channels, De Valois & De Valois 1988) → V2/V4 (contour and texture) → IT (object features, Tanaka 1996) is the canonical primate ventral-stream description and the empirical anchor for HMAX and modern DNN models of object recognition. The user's program follows this chain directly in its three-layer reference architecture (program §3).

**Connection to `dicarlo2012_object_recognition`.** DiCarlo et al.'s ventral-stream-as-DNN framework starts from primate V1 as a Gabor-channel filter bank — citing exactly the De Valoises' synthesis as the V1 description that modern DNN models replicate at their first convolutional layer. The user's program inherits the DiCarlo framing: a hierarchical feedforward backbone whose first layer is Gabor-like, whose intermediate layers progressively pool over space and abstract over features, and whose top layer is object-selective. The De Valoises' book is the quantitative V1 description that makes this framing biologically grounded rather than a generic deep-learning convenience.

**Connection to `felleman_vanessen1991_hierarchical_cortex`.** Felleman & Van Essen catalogue the hierarchical structure of primate visual cortex in which V1 is the entry point and IT/PFC the endpoints. The De Valoises' book describes the quantitative computation performed by that entry point. Together they establish the V1-level computation (Gabor-channel decomposition) and the macroscopic hierarchy (cortical area hierarchy with reciprocal connections) that the user's program's bidirectional, hierarchical, parallel memory architecture (program §3) instantiates in artificial form.

**Gabor-as-uncertainty-bound → why the V1 representation is universal.** The book emphasises (drawing on Daugman 1985) that Gabor functions saturate the joint space / spatial-frequency uncertainty bound — they are the optimal filters for representing structure that is local in both space and frequency. This is the formal reason that *any* visual system, biological or artificial, that must process structure at multiple scales over multiple positions converges on Gabor-like filters at its earliest layers. CNN-trained-on-images convergence to Gabor first-layer filters (Krizhevsky 2012; Yamins & DiCarlo 2016, in seed) is not a coincidence — it is the optimal solution to the joint space-frequency localisation problem that biological vision also solved. The user's program inherits this argument: a ViT patch projection that does not produce a Gabor-like channel decomposition is sub-optimal under the same uncertainty bound and should be expected to underperform a biologically-aligned architecture, all else equal.

**Limits the program inherits.** Like the Hubel & Wiesel papers, the De Valoises' book supports a strictly feedforward, quasi-linear account of V1; the recurrent and top-down extensions that the user's program insists on (program §3, bidirectional hierarchical feedback) are layered *on top of* the Gabor-channel framework, not embedded in it. The book is therefore the *feedforward kernel* of the user's V1-aligned architecture; the recurrent dynamics, normalisation, attention, and top-down feedback are additional commitments that come from other parts of the literature (Reynolds & Heeger 2009; Kietzmann et al. 2019; Rao & Ballard 1999; Bastos et al. 2012, all in seed). The user's program reads the relationship as the De Valoises themselves do: the Gabor-channel framework is the empirically-validated first-order description of V1, and a complete primate-aligned visual architecture builds recurrence, normalisation, and feedback on top of it.

**Quantitative grounding for patch sizes and channel counts.** A practical implication for the user's program: the book's quantitative description of macaque V1 — receptive fields of ~0.05–0.5° near the fovea, orientation columns of ~10–20° steps, ~6 octaves of spatial-frequency coverage, ~30–40° orientation bandwidth — provides numerical grounding for the patch sizes, channel counts, and feature-bandwidth budgets of the user's architecture. A ViT-style patch grid of 16×16 pixels on a standard ImageNet image corresponds roughly to ~1° of visual angle per patch, which is a *coarser* spatial sampling than foveal macaque V1 — suggesting that an even finer patch grid (or a non-uniform foveated grid) would be more biologically aligned for the user's program. The De Valoises' book is the source of these numerical anchors.

**Future extensions of the program suggested by the book.** Two natural extensions are: (i) explicit factorisation of patch-token channels along the *named* feature dimensions (orientation, spatial frequency, phase, eye-of-origin, colour-opponent vs. luminance) that primate V1 carries, rather than the generic linear projection used in current ViTs and in the published Recurrent ViT; and (ii) a foveated patch grid with finer spatial sampling near the centre, matching the cortical-magnification factor that the De Valoises and Hubel & Wiesel both document. Either extension would be a step toward the *quantitatively primate-aligned* version of the user's architecture that the program ultimately envisages.

**Justifying multi-scale processing in the GridCell-RNN hierarchy.** The book's demonstration that V1 spans ~6 octaves of spatial frequency through a population of cells with ~1-octave bandwidths is the empirical justification for the *multi-scale* aspect of the user's hierarchical memory architecture (program §3). PRISM v1 and v2's hierarchical conv backbones, and the user's three-layer reference design, implement multi-scale processing through spatial-resolution reduction in the descending-projection conv stack — biologically aligned with the V1-to-V2-to-V4 progression in which receptive fields grow and preferred spatial frequencies drop. The De Valoises' quantitative description of the V1 spatial-frequency range and bandwidth pattern is the entry point for this entire multi-scale progression in primate cortex.

**Phase and quadrature pairs → motion energy → video work.** The book's explicit construction of complex cells as energy detectors over pairs of quadrature-phase simple cells is the direct biological precedent for the motion-energy and binocular-energy models that subsequent computational vision relies on. For the user's video-autoencoding work (program §6, "Video VAE") and the change-detection benchmarks of the Recurrent ViT (2502.10955), motion-sensitive features at the V1 level — energy-detected from quadrature-phase Gabor pairs across time — are the substrate over which all higher-level temporal processing must operate. The De Valoises' framework supplies the V1-level account; the recurrence of the user's GridCell-RNN supplies the temporal integration that the V1 energy detectors feed into.

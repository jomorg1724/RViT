# Neuromodulation of Persistent Activity and Working Memory Circuitry in Primate Prefrontal Cortex by Muscarinic Receptors

## 1. Abstract

Cholinergic neuromodulation plays a vital role in shaping the physiology and function of cerebral cortex. Acetylcholine (ACh) released from basal-forebrain afferents influences brain-state transitions, gates cortical sensory responses, and shapes both the generation and the maintenance of persistent activity in primate prefrontal cortex (PFC). This review consolidates current understanding of how muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs) — the slow, G-protein-coupled arm of the cholinergic system — regulate PFC engagement during working-memory tasks. The authors summarise the laminar and cell-type-specific localisation of muscarinic receptor subtypes (M1, M2, M4) in macaque dorsolateral PFC; review the behavioural effects of muscarinic agonists and antagonists on arousal, spatial working memory, oculomotor control, and antisaccade performance; and synthesise recent primate iontophoretic-recording experiments showing that M1 stimulation and blockade exert heterogeneous, dose-dependent, and cell-type-specific effects on the directional persistent activity of dlPFC "delay" cells. Two findings dominate: (1) M1 receptors are not uniformly excitatory in primate PFC — different M1-positive cell populations show *opposite* responses to the same agonist, with a sub-population whose persistent activity is *enhanced* by M1 blockade rather than impaired, contrary to the rodent literature; (2) the M1 modulation interacts with the cell's task-tuning state, so the same pharmacological perturbation amplifies preferred-direction firing in some cells and suppresses non-preferred firing in others — implementing a *gain-and-contrast* operation on the memory-field rather than a uniform multiplicative gain. The authors close by arguing that muscarinic agents act as state-dependent precision regulators of PFC working-memory circuitry and that this primate-specific pharmacological profile is critical for designing M1-targeted therapies in schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease.

## 2. Why this matters for us

This review supplies the *neuromodulatory layer* that sits on top of the persistent-activity working-memory substrate established by [funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc](funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc.md), [goldman_rakic1995_cellular_wm](goldman_rakic1995_cellular_wm.md), and [constantinidis2018_persistent_activity](constantinidis2018_persistent_activity.md). The user's program (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md`) commits to multi-compartmental memory states maintained by recurrent gating; but persistent activity in vivo is *not* a fixed substrate — it is dynamically tuned by cholinergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic modulators that set the gain, the duration, and the directional contrast of the memory field. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 is the most up-to-date primate-specific synthesis of how *one* of those modulators (ACh, via M1 muscarinic receptors) shapes that substrate. The relevance is threefold. First, the paper licenses a specific mapping between the user's PRISM v1/v2 FiLM modulation primitive (`THESIS.md` §2.4) and ACh-style gain control: muscarinic γ corresponds to a state-dependent multiplicative scalar that the network applies to the memory cell's update. Second, the heterogeneity finding — M1 blockade amplifies some cells, suppresses others — supplies the empirical basis for the *contrast-sharpening* (rather than uniform-gain) account of attention/precision that complements [thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation](thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation.md). Third, the existence of a distinct *neuromodulator* signal at the working-memory site is direct evidence for a separable "neuromodulatory hub" feeding the memory hub — a concrete biological instance of the user's `multi-hub-multi-objective-system` (`competition-emergent-predictive-coding`).

## 3. Key claims

1. Acetylcholine reaches dlPFC via diffuse projections from the basal-forebrain Ch4 (nucleus basalis of Meynert) cluster, with both fast nicotinic and slow muscarinic receptors expressed on PFC pyramidal cells and interneurons.
2. Muscarinic M1 receptors are densely expressed in primate dlPFC, particularly on layer-III pyramidal neurons — the cells that implement recurrent excitation supporting persistent activity in microcircuit models.
3. Systemic muscarinic antagonism (scopolamine, atropine) produces robust working-memory and antisaccade deficits in macaques, paralleling cognitive impairment seen in human cholinergic-blockade studies and in Alzheimer's disease.
4. Iontophoretic application of M1 agonists at the recording site produces *heterogeneous*, dose-dependent effects on dlPFC persistent activity: a subset of delay cells show enhanced preferred-direction firing; another subset show suppression at all directions; a third subset show selective enhancement of non-preferred-direction firing (i.e., loss of directional contrast).
5. M1 *blockade* (telenzepine, pirenzepine) does not uniformly impair persistent activity — contrary to the prevailing rodent-derived picture — and in a sub-population of cells M1 blockade *enhances* directional persistent activity. The primate pharmacology is therefore not a straightforward "more ACh, better memory" relationship.
6. The net behavioural effect of muscarinic manipulation depends on which cell sub-population dominates the recorded population and on the baseline cholinergic tone — a state-dependence that mirrors the inverted-U dose-response curve characterised earlier for D1 dopaminergic modulation of the same circuit.
7. Differences between primate and rodent M1 pharmacology (uniform enhancement in rodents vs. heterogeneous, cell-type-specific effects in primates) imply that primate dlPFC has evolved a more finely partitioned muscarinic micro-pharmacology, likely reflecting expansion of the cell-type repertoire in granular primate PFC.
8. The pharmacological heterogeneity is best understood as a *gain-and-contrast* control rather than a global gain: muscarinic tone modulates both the firing rate of delay cells and the *width* / *selectivity* of their memory field, implementing a precision-weighting operation on the working-memory representation.
9. M1-selective agonists and positive allosteric modulators are promising therapeutic targets for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia and Alzheimer's, but designing them requires accounting for cell-type-specific primate pharmacology rather than extrapolating from rodent behavioural assays.

## 4. Methods

This is a review article. The authors integrate evidence from four sources.

**Anatomy and receptor distribution.** Immunohistochemical and autoradiographic studies of mAChR subtype distribution in primate cortex (Disney & Aoki 2008; Disney et al. 2006; Disney & Reynolds 2014; Mrzljak et al. 1993; Volpicelli & Levey 2004) identify the laminar and cell-type localisation of M1, M2, M3, and M4 receptors in macaque dlPFC. The authors place particular weight on cell-type-specific M1 expression on parvalbumin-positive interneurons and on layer-III pyramidal cells, which together constitute the recurrent-excitation/inhibition motif that microcircuit models of persistent activity rely on (Goldman-Rakic 1995; Compte, Brunel, Goldman-Rakic & Wang 2000).

**Systemic pharmacological behavioural studies.** A large literature in macaques and humans administers muscarinic agonists (oxotremorine, pilocarpine, xanomeline) or antagonists (scopolamine, atropine, telenzepine, pirenzepine) before working-memory tasks: the oculomotor delayed-response paradigm of [funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc](funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc.md), delayed match-to-sample (DMS), delayed match-to-position, the antisaccade task (Everling & Fischer 1998), and combined Posner-type spatial-cueing tasks (Davidson & Marrocco 2000). Accuracy, reaction time, and error patterns are read off across delay durations and load conditions. The review consolidates effect sizes across studies to argue that muscarinic blockade produces a *delay-length-dependent* and *load-dependent* deficit — exactly the signature expected if maintenance, not encoding, is the limiting step.

**Iontophoretic single-unit recording.** This is the technically critical method underpinning the heterogeneity claim. In awake macaques performing ODR or DMS, a recording electrode is paired with a micropipette through which a small dose of an mAChR agonist or antagonist is iontophoretically ejected at the recording site while the cell's task-related firing is measured (Major et al. 2015, 2018; Vijayraghavan et al. 2018; Galvin et al. 2020). The local ejection isolates the pharmacological effect at the recorded cell from systemic and indirect-network effects, and the agent's dose is controlled by ejection current. Cells are classified as "delay cells" using the same ANOVA-based directional-tuning criterion as [funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc](funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc.md), and the agonist-induced change in preferred-vs-non-preferred firing is the primary read-out.

**Cellular biophysics and signalling.** The review draws on biophysical work characterising the M1 downstream cascade — Gq-coupled, phospholipase C activation, PIP2 hydrolysis, closure of KCNQ-type M-current K⁺ channels (Brown & Adams 1980; Brown 2010) — to reconcile heterogeneous physiological effects with the underlying single-channel pharmacology. Closure of the M-current depolarises the resting potential and prolongs after-depolarisations, supporting longer persistent firing in pyramidal cells but amplifying inhibitory output in PV+ interneurons.

The review's structural argument has three parts: receptor localisation → behavioural pharmacology → single-unit microcircuit effects. The authors integrate across these levels to argue that the same muscarinic signal can produce opposing physiological outcomes in different cell sub-populations because the downstream effector (M-current K⁺ channel) differentially shapes excitability and recurrent-network dynamics depending on the cell's role in the microcircuit (recurrent-excitation pyramidal cell vs. inhibitory parvalbumin interneuron vs. somatostatin interneuron). The closing therapeutic discussion is grounded in clinical pharmacology of M1-selective allosteric modulators and dual M1/M4 agonists (xanomeline, the active component of KarXT for schizophrenia).

## 5. Results

The review consolidates quantitative findings from the primary literature it surveys.

- **Receptor distribution.** M1 receptors are expressed at high density on layer-III pyramidal cells of macaque dlPFC and on a subset of parvalbumin-positive (PV+) interneurons. M2 receptors are more abundantly expressed on layer-V/VI pyramidal cells and on cholinergic afferents themselves (where they act as autoreceptors). M4 receptors are sparser and largely cell-type-restricted.
- **Behavioural pharmacology.** Systemic scopolamine produces dose-dependent working-memory deficits in macaques performing ODR, with delay-length-dependent error rates: errors are larger at long delays (≥6 s), implicating maintenance rather than encoding. Effect sizes are comparable to those produced by reversible cooling of dlPFC ([funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc](funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc.md)).
- **M1 iontophoresis — heterogeneity.** Of dlPFC delay cells tested with local M1 agonist iontophoresis, roughly one-third show enhanced preferred-direction firing (the canonical "more ACh, better memory" pattern), roughly one-third show suppression at all directions, and roughly one-third show selective enhancement of non-preferred-direction firing — i.e., loss of memory-field selectivity. The exact fractions vary across cited studies but the three-way split is consistent.
- **M1 blockade — non-uniform.** Telenzepine and pirenzepine produce mixed effects: in some cells, blockade reduces directional delay firing (consistent with rodent data and with cognitive deficits seen behaviourally); in other cells, blockade *enhances* directional contrast, sharpening the memory field.
- **Dose dependence.** Effects on persistent activity are non-monotonic in dose, with intermediate doses producing the strongest enhancement of directional contrast and high doses degrading it — an inverted-U pattern analogous to the D1 dopaminergic curve in the same circuit (Vijayraghavan et al. 2007; Williams & Goldman-Rakic 1995).
- **Cross-species divergence.** Rodent M1 pharmacology shows uniform enhancement of cortical persistent activity by M1 agonists; primate pharmacology shows the heterogeneous three-way split described above. The cross-species divergence is one of the review's central data points.
- **Downstream signalling.** M1 activation suppresses the KCNQ-type M-current K⁺ channel via Gq → PLC → PIP2 hydrolysis. The suppression depolarises the resting potential and prolongs after-depolarisations, which in recurrent-excitation pyramidal cells supports longer persistent firing but in PV+ interneurons amplifies inhibitory output and thereby *suppresses* downstream pyramidal firing — providing a candidate cellular explanation for the heterogeneous net effect.
- **Therapeutic landscape.** M1-selective allosteric modulators (e.g., VU0453595) and dual M1/M4 agonists (e.g., xanomeline, the active component of the schizophrenia drug KarXT) are emerging therapeutic candidates whose behavioural effects in primate models can be more accurately predicted by accounting for the heterogeneous cell-type pharmacology than by uniform-gain models.
- **Anti-saccade and cognitive-control coupling.** Beyond pure ODR maintenance, muscarinic manipulation specifically degrades anti-saccade performance (the task in which the monkey must suppress a prepotent saccade toward a peripheral cue and instead saccade away from it). This implicates muscarinic tone in *top-down inhibitory control* exerted by dlPFC on the oculomotor system, not just in delay-period maintenance. The deficit pattern (preserved pro-saccade, impaired anti-saccade) is consistent with selective disruption of the dlPFC → frontal-eye-field / superior-colliculus suppressive projection.
- **Interaction with arousal state.** Behavioural deficits induced by systemic scopolamine interact with the monkey's baseline arousal/engagement: deficits are larger in early-session trials when arousal is sub-optimal and partially attenuated in mid-session high-engagement blocks. This is consistent with cholinergic tone interacting with noradrenergic / α2A-driven arousal, supporting a multi-modulator view of PFC state control.
- **Schizophrenia and Alzheimer's mapping.** Post-mortem dlPFC tissue from schizophrenia patients shows decreased M1 receptor density (Dean et al. 2002); Alzheimer's tissue shows broader cholinergic deficit including basal-forebrain Ch4 cell loss. The review uses these endpoints to argue that the heterogeneous primate M1 pharmacology has direct translational implications: which sub-population dominates the deficit determines which therapeutic regime is appropriate.

## 6. Critique / limitations

- As a **review**, the paper consolidates rather than extends the empirical record; the strongest novel claims (heterogeneity of M1 effects, primate–rodent divergence) rest on a handful of primary papers from the Arnsten and Everling labs and have not been independently replicated at the scale of, e.g., the persistent-activity dataset itself.
- The iontophoretic results that anchor the heterogeneity claim are obtained in *anaesthetised* or lightly-task-engaged primates in some cases; the precise behavioural-state dependence of the M1 effect remains under-characterised, and the relationship between the heterogeneous single-cell effects and the *systemic* behavioural deficits is inferential rather than mechanistically demonstrated.
- The review's central pharmacological-cellular bridge — that M1-suppression of the M-current produces *opposite* effects in pyramidal vs. PV+ cells — is a hypothesis supported by biophysical modelling and indirect evidence rather than by simultaneous cell-type-identified recordings. The authors do not resolve whether the three-way split in delay-cell responses corresponds cleanly to three cell types or to a continuum of micro-circuit positions.
- The review focuses almost exclusively on M1 (and to a lesser extent M2); M3, M4, and M5 modulation of PFC are treated only briefly, leaving open the possibility that the heterogeneity reflects mixed M1/M4 actions rather than M1 alone — particularly given that xanomeline and KarXT are dual M1/M4 agonists.
- The cross-species comparison (rodent uniform enhancement vs. primate heterogeneity) is qualitative; quantitative meta-analysis with matched paradigms and doses across species is not provided.
- The review does not engage substantively with the activity-silent / synaptic-working-memory alternative ([mongillo2008_synaptic_wm](mongillo2008_synaptic_wm.md)). If a non-trivial fraction of working memory is held in short-term synaptic facilitation rather than in persistent firing, the muscarinic modulation of firing may be only a partial window onto the relevant mechanism.
- The therapeutic discussion is necessarily speculative — the cell-type-specific account predicts that some patients will benefit and others will be impaired by the *same* M1-agonist therapy, but the review does not specify the biomarkers that would distinguish the two populations clinically.
- Effects of basal-forebrain stimulation or lesion on PFC persistent activity are reviewed only sketchily; the causal chain from Ch4 firing → ACh release → PFC dynamics is not closed within this paper and would require integration with optogenetic work in rodents (Chen, Sugihara & Sur 2015) and emerging primate viral tools.

## 7. Connection to our work

This paper is the **neuromodulatory layer** of the persistent-activity story that the user's architectural program rests on. The previous papers in this cluster — [funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc](funahashi1989_mnemonic_dlpfc.md), [goldman_rakic1995_cellular_wm](goldman_rakic1995_cellular_wm.md), [constantinidis2018_persistent_activity](constantinidis2018_persistent_activity.md), [riley_constantinidis2016_pfc_persistent](riley_constantinidis2016_pfc_persistent.md) — establish *that* PFC neurons sustain a content-specific representation across a delay. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 establishes *how that representation is dynamically tuned*: not by the recurrent network alone, but by a slow, diffuse, state-dependent neuromodulatory signal that sets the gain, contrast, and duration of the memory field. For the user's program, four connections are load-bearing.

**Connection 1: Neuromodulatory γ as ACh-style state-dependent gain — the FiLM primitive in PRISM.** PRISM v1's FiLM modulation (`THESIS.md` §2.4) and PRISM v2's hierarchical FiLM (`PRISM_V2_PROPOSAL.md` §3.4) apply a per-channel multiplicative scalar γ (and additive offset β) to the feature stack. The interpretation has so far been "context-dependent gating". Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 supplies a concrete biological referent: γ is the muscarinic gain on dlPFC delay cells, set by basal-forebrain cholinergic tone, that determines whether the memory cell's representation is sharpened or blurred. The non-monotonic (inverted-U) dose-response means γ is bounded and state-dependent, supporting the architectural choice to constrain FiLM γ to a moderate range rather than allowing unbounded multiplicative amplification. The cross-species divergence (uniform in rodent, heterogeneous in primate) implies that *learnable per-channel* γ — rather than a global scalar — is the biologically correct primate primitive, supporting PRISM v2's hierarchical (per-layer, per-channel) FiLM over a single global modulator.

**Connection 2: Heterogeneous gain → contrast-sharpening, not uniform amplification.** The single most important finding for our work is that M1 modulation does not produce uniform gain — it produces *opposite* effects in different cell sub-populations, with one sub-population enhanced and another suppressed by the same pharmacological perturbation. This is the cellular substrate of contrast-sharpening / precision-weighting, distinct from divisive normalisation. The Feedback Transformer (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md` §1) integrates feedback via element-wise *Hadamard* products on Q/K/V projections — a multiplicative operation that, on a per-token basis, can amplify some tokens while suppressing others depending on the sign of the feedback contribution. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 is the biological warrant for this asymmetric multiplicative integration: real cortex implements precisely such heterogeneous gain via cell-type-specific muscarinic pharmacology. The user's choice of multiplicative (Hadamard) over additive feedback in the Feedback Transformer (Recurrent ViT §6.7) is supported by the muscarinic data more directly than by the additive-feedback variant.

**Connection 3: Neuromodulator as a separable hub in the multi-hub architecture.** The user's `multi-hub-multi-objective-system` commits to separate hubs (MSI, RL, VAE, etc.) whose outputs are integrated into a central self-attention computation. Acetylcholine is exactly such a *separable* signal in the brain: it is generated by the Ch4 basal-forebrain cluster, projects diffusely to cortex, and provides a state-dependent gain signal that the cortical microcircuits must integrate with their bottom-up sensory drive. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 demonstrates this integration at the single-cell level — the same dlPFC delay cell receives both task-relevant cue input (sensory hub) and modulatory M1 drive (cholinergic hub) and combines them multiplicatively. This is a concrete neurobiological instance of the user's multi-hub competition: the cholinergic hub does not compete *for the same output channel* as the sensory hub; rather, it modulates the gain at which the sensory hub's signal is held in working memory. The architectural analogue is a *modulator hub* whose Q/K/V projections are read by the central Feedback Transformer not as competing content but as scaling factors — naturally implemented as a FiLM-like multiplicative term in the attention computation.

**Connection 4: State-dependent inverted-U → bounded, optimally-tuned modulation.** Both the dopaminergic D1 modulation (Vijayraghavan et al. 2007) and the muscarinic M1 modulation reviewed here follow an *inverted-U* dose-response on PFC persistent activity: too little is bad, too much is also bad, optimal performance lies in an intermediate range. This is empirical justification for architectures that *learn* the gain rather than maximising it. PRISM v2's chrono-initialised forget-gate biases (citing Tallec & Ollivier 2018; see `PRISM_V2_PROPOSAL.md` §3.3) are the slow-memory analogue: forget-gate values that are too high (no decay) overfit; too low (immediate decay) erase content; an intermediate optimum exists and must be learned. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 generalises this principle from forget gates to *all neuromodulatory inputs*: the architectural commitment should be to learn the gain, with regularisation that prevents both saturation and silencing.

**Bridge to [thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation](thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation.md).** Thiele & Bellgrove treat all four major neuromodulators (ACh, NA, DA, 5-HT) at the level of attention. Vijayraghavan & Everling 2021 zooms in on the ACh / muscarinic arm at the working-memory site. Together they license the design pattern of *separate modulatory pathways for distinct cognitive regimes*: ACh / muscarinic for working-memory gain (this paper), NA / α2A for distraction resistance and arousal (Thiele & Bellgrove), DA / D1 for reward-guided priority (Thiele & Bellgrove and [thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation](thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation.md)). The user's program should at minimum acknowledge that a *single* FiLM modulator collapses what biology implements as a small zoo of dissociable modulatory channels, and PRISM v2 or a successor could profitably factorise the FiLM input into multiple modulator "voices" each contributing its own γ.

**Bridge to PRISM's inner variational loop.** PRISM v1's inner inference loop (`THESIS.md` §2.8) iteratively refines a posterior over the memory state given the current observation. The cellular analogue is the *online* updating of dlPFC delay activity in response to ongoing cholinergic tone: each iontophoretic dose of an M1 agonist reshapes the directional persistent activity within seconds. The biological time-scale (seconds, matched to delay duration) is consistent with PRISM's inner-loop time-scale (multiple inference steps per frame). The user's framing of the inner loop as variational free-energy minimisation could be sharpened by reading the muscarinic literature as setting the *precision* of the prior over the memory state — exactly the role the precision-weighting concept plays in predictive-coding (Bastos 2012; Friston 2010) and in [thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation](thiele_bellgrove2018_neuromodulation.md).

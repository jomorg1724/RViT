# Warning! Dopaminergic modulation of the superior colliculus

## 1. Abstract

> "Recent work by Bolton *et al.* describes a dopaminergic input to the superior colliculus (SC) from the zona incerta, as well as the organization of D1- and D2-receptor expression in the SC. We discuss a potential role for this input in modulating SC-mediated behavior, particularly in response to aversive stimuli." (Essig & Felsen 2016, *Trends in Neurosciences* 39(1):2–4, abstract; retrieved from PMC4866502 via NCBI eutils.)

The piece is a *TINS* Spotlight / Forum article (3 pages) commenting on [bolton2015_dopamine_sc](research_db/papers/bolton2015_dopamine_sc.md) (*Cell Reports* 13(5):1003–1015). It is not a primary-data paper; it is an interpretive synthesis of Bolton et al.'s anatomical and slice-physiology results, raising — but not testing — a specific hypothesis about how a previously-overlooked dopaminergic input to the SC might shape defensive orienting behavior.

## 2. Why this matters for us

The user's program ([threads/the_user_architectural_program.md](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md) §5) places an RL hub alongside MSI and VAE hubs, all competing for control of a shared self-attention substrate. [glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe](research_db/papers/glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe.md) supplies the canonical *forward* limb: dopamine as a reward-prediction-error teaching signal in cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamic loops. Essig & Felsen 2016 supplies a *complementary subcortical limb*: a dopaminergic projection — from zona incerta A13, *not* SNc/VTA — that directly modulates the superior colliculus, the canonical priority-map substrate ([krauzlis2013_sc_attention](research_db/papers/krauzlis2013_sc_attention.md)). For the user's architecture, this is the missing biological warrant that the RL hub's neuromodulatory signal can reach the attention substrate *directly*, without traversing the corticostriatal route — making it concrete that "RL hub feedback into self-attention" has a literal anatomical homolog rather than being a purely computational analogy. It is also the source identifier for an *aversive* / defensive dopaminergic channel that complements the reward-RPE story, supporting a richer multi-channel design in which the RL hub emits more than one signed signal into the attention substrate.

## 3. Key claims

1. The superior colliculus expresses both D1 (Drd1a) and D2 (Drd2) dopamine-receptor transcripts at levels comparable to canonical glutamate-receptor transcripts — i.e., the SC is a *bona fide* dopaminoceptive structure, not a marginal target.
2. D1- and D2-expressing SC neurons are *laminarly segregated*: D1 neurons predominantly in superficial layers (visual sensory layers); D2 neurons predominantly in intermediate layers (sensorimotor / motor layers). The lamination implies dopamine influences sensory and motor stages of SC processing through different receptor mechanisms.
3. The *source* of SC dopamine is not the canonical SNc (A9) or VTA (A10), but **A13, a dopaminergic subregion of the zona incerta** (a diencephalic structure), with a small contribution from locus coeruleus. This is anatomically and functionally surprising and distinguishes SC dopamine from striatal dopamine.
4. A13 terminals in SC lack the dopamine transporter (DAT), unlike SNc/VTA terminals. This implies *spillover* / volume-transmission rather than tight synaptic recycling — dopamine in SC acts more as a slow neuromodulator than as a fast point-to-point signal.
5. *In vitro* whole-cell recordings show that dopamine application robustly **inhibits** both D1- and D2-expressing SC neurons. This is a notable departure from the cortico-striatal pattern (where D1 = facilitatory, D2 = suppressive). Authors propose net inhibition is the dominant effect.
6. A13 preferentially innervates **medial SC**, which represents the upper visual field and (in rodents) drives **avoidance / defensive orienting** to overhead aversive stimuli (e.g., looming predators). Lateral SC, which drives **approach** to lower-visual-field appetitive stimuli, receives input from *other* zona incerta subregions.
7. A13 also projects to the **periaqueductal gray (PAG)**, another defensive-behavior hub, and a D2-antagonist (sulpiride) infused into either SC or PAG elicits similar avoidance behavior. The authors *hypothesize* that A13 coordinates avoidance signaling jointly across SC and PAG.
8. The functional dichotomy of dopamine sources is striking: SNc/VTA dopamine encodes reward-prediction error and trains action selection via striatum; A13 dopamine appears to encode (or at least modulate) *aversive* / defensive signals at midbrain orienting hubs. Dopamine is not a monolithic "reward" signal but a *multi-source neuromodulator* with distinct functional channels.
9. Open question raised by the authors: how does the dopaminergic input *to* SC relate to the input from SC *to* dopaminergic regions (Redgrave & Gurney 2006's short-latency tectonigral signal)? The bidirectional SC–dopamine relationship is structurally important and underexplored.
10. Methodologically: investigating SC neuromodulation requires combining receptor mapping (where the receptors are), anatomical tracing (where the dopamine comes from), slice physiology (what the receptors do at the single-cell level), and behavioral pharmacology (what the system does at the organismal level). The piece is a worked example of how each of these alone is insufficient and how the *conjunction* identifies the candidate functional channel — a methodological lesson for the user's program, where matching artificial RL-hub manipulations onto biological dopaminergic perturbations requires the same multi-level triangulation.

## 4. Methods

The piece is interpretive, not experimental. Essig & Felsen do not generate new data; they re-narrate Bolton et al. (2015) and integrate it with the prior literature on SC functional architecture. The interpretive method is *triangulation* — combining circuit anatomy, receptor laminar distribution, slice physiology, and rodent behavior into a single working hypothesis about A13 dopamine's functional role.

The underlying methods they summarize, from Bolton et al. (2015) [bolton2015_dopamine_sc](research_db/papers/bolton2015_dopamine_sc.md), are:

- **RNA-seq** in rat SC quantifying *Drd1a* and *Drd2* transcript abundance against glutamate-receptor reference transcripts (NMDAR subunit *Grin1*, AMPAR subunit *Gria1*). The reference comparison is the critical methodological move: it converts a qualitative "the receptor is present" claim into a quantitative "the receptor is present at a level comparable to the canonical fast-synaptic glutamate machinery," which is what licenses the claim that the SC is genuinely dopaminoceptive rather than marginally so.
- **Transgenic reporter mice** — Drd1a-tdTomato and Drd2-EGFP lines (the canonical BAC-transgenic reporters from the Heintz/GENSAT project), crossed with VGAT-Venus to mark GABAergic neurons. Fluorescence imaging of fixed SC sections gives the laminar and cell-type-specific distribution of D1 vs D2 expression and whether each population is inhibitory or excitatory.
- **Retrograde tracing** (cholera toxin B and fluorescent retrobeads) injected into SC, then combined with immunohistochemistry for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH; the rate-limiting enzyme for dopamine synthesis) and the dopamine transporter (DAT). Retrograde label + TH co-staining identifies dopaminergic SC-projecting cell bodies; TH+/DAT− terminal staining in SC corroborates the A13 origin (since A13 is uniquely DAT-negative among dopaminergic cell groups).
- **Whole-cell patch-clamp** in acute SC slices, with bath application of dopamine or D1/D2-selective agonists / antagonists (SKF-38393, quinpirole, SCH-23390, sulpiride), recording from genetically identified D1+ or D2+ neurons. Outcome measures: resting membrane potential, action-potential firing rate, and synaptically evoked excitatory / inhibitory postsynaptic currents.

Essig & Felsen then *layer in* complementary evidence from the prior literature: Comoli et al. (2012) on segregated lateral/medial SC input subserving approach/avoidance; Messanvi et al. (2013) on the A13 → PAG projection; Muthuraju et al. (2016) on D2-antagonist-induced avoidance behavior in SC and PAG; Redgrave & Gurney (2006) on the *reverse* SC → dopamine short-latency salience signal; and Dean et al. (1989), the classic *TINS* paper that established the medial/lateral SC dichotomy as "event" (orienting) vs "emergency" (defensive) systems.

The argument structure is: anatomy (A13 → medial SC) + receptor distribution (D1 superficial, D2 intermediate) + physiology (uniform dopaminergic inhibition) + complementary projection (A13 → PAG) + pharmacology (D2-antagonist drives avoidance) → tentative conclusion that A13 → SC dopamine is part of a midbrain defensive-orienting system. Each individual leg of this argument is weak (slice physiology, single pharmacological manipulation, indirect behavioral inference); the *conjunction* is what carries the rhetorical weight.

The piece's *Figure 1* is a schematic that captures the proposed circuit in a single image: A13 (zona incerta) projecting in parallel to medial SC and to dorsolateral PAG, with both midbrain hubs jointly producing an avoidance response to an overhead aversive stimulus (depicted as a looming predator). The schematic deliberately suppresses the cortical inputs to SC and the SNc → striatum pathway, isolating the *non-canonical* dopaminergic limb that the piece is advocating attention to. This rhetorical move — isolating the unfamiliar circuit by suppressing the well-known one — is characteristic of *TINS* Spotlight articles and is methodologically relevant for the user: the user's program likewise frames the multi-hub system by isolating individual feedback channels (e.g., Touchpoint 1 below isolates the A13-like *direct* RL-to-attention path from the canonical *indirect* CBGTC path).

## 5. Results

As a Forum article, the piece has no new results of its own. It consolidates the following findings from Bolton et al. (2015) and the cited supporting literature, organized along the receptor-localization → input-source → physiology → behavior chain:

- *Drd1a* and *Drd2* transcripts are present in rat SC at levels comparable to glutamate-receptor transcripts (Bolton et al. 2015 RNA-seq).
- D1-expressing neurons are concentrated in **superficial SC layers** (stratum griseum superficiale); a *majority* of these are GABAergic (overlap with VGAT-Venus).
- D2-expressing neurons are concentrated in **intermediate SC layers** (stratum griseum intermediale); a *minority* of these are GABAergic — most are glutamatergic / excitatory.
- Retrograde labeling from SC produces **no labeling in SNc (A9) or VTA (A10)**, but produces clear labeling in **A13 / zona incerta** and a smaller signal in locus coeruleus.
- A13 dopaminergic terminals in SC are **DAT-negative**, consistent with the fact that A13 is the only known dopaminergic cell group that does not express DAT.
- Bath-applied dopamine **inhibits both D1- and D2-expressing SC neurons** (Bolton et al. slice recordings) — a uniform inhibitory effect, in contrast to the bidirectional D1-LTP / D2-LTD pattern in striatum.
- A13 axons terminate preferentially in **medial SC** (upper visual field representation), the avoidance-driving subregion of the rodent SC.
- Local infusion of sulpiride (D2-antagonist) into either SC or PAG elicits similar **avoidance behavior** in rats (Muthuraju et al. 2016) — consistent with the idea that tonic D2-mediated inhibition normally *suppresses* avoidance, and removing it releases the defensive response.
- A13 sends collateral projections to **PAG**, supporting a model in which A13 coordinates avoidance computations across two midbrain hubs (SC + PAG).
- Quantitative implications: in the user's terms, A13 → SC dopamine acts on the *priority map directly*, with an estimated time constant of seconds (volume-transmission slow neuromodulation) rather than tens of milliseconds (synaptic transmission). The receptor laminar segregation (D1 superficial sensory layers; D2 intermediate sensorimotor layers) implies dopamine differentially shapes the *visual* and the *motor* readouts of SC, not a single unitary modulation — a richer functional repertoire than the abstract reward-RPE picture would predict.

## 6. Critique / limitations

The piece is openly *speculative*: the authors propose, but do not test, the hypothesis that A13 → SC dopamine encodes an aversive / defensive signal. Several caveats need to be foregrounded for the user's program.

- **No in-vivo physiology.** Everything cellular is from acute slices in Bolton et al. The behavior of A13 → SC during awake orienting — and whether A13 neurons phasically fire to predators, looming stimuli, or aversive cues — is unknown as of 2016. (Some of this gap has since been filled, e.g., by work on superior-colliculus circuits for looming detection in mouse, but the specific A13 firing pattern remains underspecified.)
- **Rodent-only.** All data are from rat / mouse SC. The primate SC has a more attention-and-saccade-centric functional profile ([krauzlis2013_sc_attention](research_db/papers/krauzlis2013_sc_attention.md)); whether A13-like input exists with the same organization in primate is not addressed.
- **Uniform inhibition is suspicious.** A "both D1 and D2 inhibit" pattern at the same neuron population is unusual and may reflect slice-physiology peculiarities (e.g., dopamine-modulated GABA release from nearby D1 GABAergic interneurons confounding the apparent D2 effect). The authors flag but do not resolve this.
- **DAT-negativity is mechanistically loaded.** Volume transmission is a *very* different signaling mode from striatal phasic dopamine. The A13 signal is likely to be slow, diffuse, and tonic / state-like — not phasic-RPE-like. Importing the canonical RPE story onto A13 → SC dopamine is therefore inappropriate without further evidence; the user should treat A13 dopamine as a *gain/state* signal, not as a TD-style teaching signal.
- **A13 ≠ a clean labeled line.** A13 receives diverse inputs (hypothalamic, brainstem, cortical) and its activity is not yet characterized in any single behavioral computation. Identifying it with "aversive RPE" or "defensive gate" is a working hypothesis that may dissolve as more is learned.
- **Approach / avoidance dichotomy is too clean.** The medial / lateral SC mapping onto avoidance / approach (Dean 1989; Comoli 2012) is supported, but the framing risks overstating a categorical split where the actual SC computation is likely a continuous priority map with reward and threat as two of many input dimensions.
- **The bidirectional SC ↔ dopamine relationship is left dangling.** Redgrave & Gurney (2006) describe an SC → SNc signal for unsigned salience / novelty; Essig & Felsen note that the A13 → SC channel is a *separate* dopaminergic flow with different anatomy. The interaction of these two flows — whether they are coordinated, competitive, or independent — is the most interesting question left open.
- **No engagement with the SNc/VTA-deficient story in primates.** The piece is rodent-focused and does not address whether the dopaminergic SC input in primate is also A13-sourced or whether the canonical SNc/VTA contribute. This matters for translating the result onto the user's primate-grounded program ([krauzlis2013_sc_attention](research_db/papers/krauzlis2013_sc_attention.md), [hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes](research_db/papers/hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes.md), which are macaque).
- **Inhibition vs disinhibition is hard to predict.** Since *both* D1 and D2 SC neurons are inhibited by dopamine, the net behavioral effect depends on which subpopulation dominates the output stage. The piece sketches an answer (medial-SC inhibition suppresses upper-field orienting, hence avoidance via *gating off* a competing approach response) but the logic chain has at least three uncertain links.
- **The "aversive dopamine" framing competes with the salience-coding literature.** Matsumoto & Hikosaka (2009) and Bromberg-Martin, Matsumoto & Hikosaka (2010) describe SNc/VTA dopamine neurons that encode *salience* or *aversive* signals rather than reward valence (Glimcher 2011 §6 critique). The A13 → SC pathway proposed here could plausibly be reframed as a *salience* channel rather than a strictly aversive one — a distinction Essig & Felsen do not draw. The user's program should treat the A13 channel as encoding "something that demands behavioral response," which collapses the appetitive/aversive distinction at the architectural level into a single high-priority gate.
- **No quantitative receptor density or release-site density.** The RNA-seq comparison establishes that *Drd1a* and *Drd2* transcripts are present at glutamate-receptor-comparable levels but does not establish that *protein* density or *functional* density is comparable. The functional impact of A13 dopamine on SC processing in vivo could be a fraction of what the transcript abundance suggests — a caveat the piece does not flag.

## 7. Connection to our work

Essig & Felsen 2016 is *short* but unusually load-bearing for the user's program because it identifies the specific neuroanatomical channel by which a dopaminergic signal can directly modulate the subcortical attention substrate. Several distinct touchpoints follow.

**Touchpoint 1: the RL hub's reward signal *directly reaches* the attention substrate.** The user's multi-hub system ([threads/the_user_architectural_program.md](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md) §5) has an RL hub whose signal modulates a shared self-attention substrate via Hadamard injection of $c^{(\text{RL})}_q, c^{(\text{RL})}_k$ vectors. The canonical CBGTC route ([haber2015_cbgtc_circuits](research_db/papers/haber2015_cbgtc_circuits.md), [glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe](research_db/papers/glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe.md), [hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes](research_db/papers/hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes.md)) routes dopamine *through* striatum and SNr before it ever reaches the SC. Essig & Felsen identify a *parallel direct channel* (A13 → SC) by which a dopaminergic neuromodulator reaches the priority map *without* a corticostriatal detour. This licenses an architectural variant in which the RL hub's signal is allowed to write *directly* into the attention substrate (multiplicative gating, no intermediate critic head) — a simpler implementation than a full actor-critic loop, justified by a real neural pathway. In Feedback Transformer terms (§1 of the program thread), this corresponds to allowing the RL hub to supply one of the $k$ feedback sources $C^{(\text{RL})}$ that the attention layer integrates directly via per-source Q/K/V projections, in addition to (or instead of) the standard pre-attention bottom-up sensory stream.

**Touchpoint 2: dopamine is multi-source, multi-functional.** [glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe](research_db/papers/glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe.md) treats phasic SNc/VTA dopamine as a near-canonical TD reward-prediction error. Essig & Felsen show that *another* dopaminergic system — A13, with different anatomy, no DAT, different polarity of effect — exists and targets a different substrate. This is direct support for the user's TAXONOMY commitment that `reward-modulated-attention` is not a single channel but a *family* of channels. For PRISM v2 and the multi-hub architecture, this means a single scalar dopamine analog is an oversimplification: the RL hub may need *at least two* output channels (a reward-bias channel and an aversive-bias channel) reflecting the SNc-vs-A13 dichotomy. The Feedback Transformer's per-source Q/K/V projections accommodate this natively.

**Touchpoint 3: volume transmission ≈ slow gain modulation, not fast teaching.** The DAT-negativity finding implies A13 dopamine acts via slow, diffuse volume transmission. This maps cleanly onto the user's `gain-modulation` concept rather than onto sharp synaptic plasticity. Architecturally, this favors implementations where the RL hub's contribution to attention is a *slowly-varying multiplicative gate over patches* (a precision-weighting term in PC-language, or a temperature-like gain in attention-language) rather than a fast per-token teaching signal. Concretely, in the Recurrent ViT (2502.10955) attention layer, the RL hub's modulation should have a longer effective integration constant than the sensory-driven Q/K computation — a soft commitment that becomes a hyperparameter prior in implementation.

**Touchpoint 4: aversive / defensive signals as a separate channel.** PRISM v1 (`THESIS.md` §2.6) trains on supervised change-detection loss; PRISM v2 (`PRISM_V2_PROPOSAL.md` §3.5) adds hierarchical-FiLM modulation. Neither has an explicit *aversive* signal. The user's program (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md` §5) envisages an RL hub trained on task reward. Essig & Felsen support a richer design in which the RL hub emits *two* signed signals — one appetitive (analog of SNc → striatum → SC disinhibition), one aversive (analog of A13 → medial SC inhibition). For an active-perception agent, this maps onto separate "explore reward-predictive patches" vs "suppress aversive / distracting patches" attention controls. This is also a candidate for distinguishing PRISM's change-flag (potentially aversive / threat-like) from PRISM's free-viewing reconstruction (potentially appetitive / curiosity-like).

**Touchpoint 5: the SC as the dopamine-attention contact point.** The user's program treats the central self-attention substrate as homologous to the SC + LIP priority map ([krauzlis2013_sc_attention](research_db/papers/krauzlis2013_sc_attention.md), [hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes](research_db/papers/hikosaka2006_bg_reward_eyes.md)). Essig & Felsen confirm that the SC itself is a *dopaminoceptive* structure — not just a downstream readout of dopamine-trained striatal output. This is the cleanest possible biological warrant for the architectural commitment that *dopaminergic (RL hub) signals enter the attention computation at the priority-map level itself*, not only via upstream gating. In the Recurrent ViT's multiplicative-feedback variant (§6.7 of the published paper), the RL hub's modulation enters attention scores directly via Hadamard product — exactly the operation A13 → SC dopamine appears to perform biologically. The published paper reports only on single-feedback-source, single-layer instances of this multiplicative variant; Essig & Felsen support extending it to a *named* RL feedback source distinct from the recurrent memory feedback, with its own timescale and its own training signal.

**Touchpoint 6: bidirectional SC ↔ dopamine relationship as competition-emergent PC.** Essig & Felsen's final question — how does A13 → SC relate to SC → dopaminergic regions (Redgrave & Gurney 2006) — is a direct echo of the user's competition-emergent-PC theory (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md` §5). Two hubs (attention hub = SC; RL hub = midbrain dopaminergic system) sending signals to each other, with each modeling the other's behavior, is exactly the inter-coalition competition the user's theory predicts must structure cortical and subcortical organization. The A13 ↔ SC ↔ SNc triangle is a candidate biological test case for the user's claim that bidirectional inter-hub feedback emerges from competitive pressure.

**Touchpoint 7: comparison with Pérez-Fernández et al. 2017 (lamprey).** [perezfernandez2017_snc_tectum](research_db/papers/perezfernandez2017_snc_tectum.md) shows that in lamprey, the *SNc* (not A13) sends a direct dopaminergic projection to the optic tectum (the SC homolog). The evolutionary comparison is striking: in lamprey the canonical RPE source (SNc) directly modulates the priority map, while in mammals this role has shifted to A13 with SNc redirected through striatum. For the user's program, the cross-species comparison suggests that the *architectural pattern* — dopaminergic modulation of priority maps — is conserved across 500M years of vertebrate evolution, even as the specific source has shifted. This is the strongest possible argument that the user's choice of "neuromodulatory feedback into attention" is a deep design pattern, not a contingent feature.

**Touchpoint 8: implications for PRISM training objectives.** A practical implication: if PRISM is to be trained under an RL objective with both reward and aversive components (e.g., correct change detection = reward; false alarm = punishment), Essig & Felsen support implementing these as *two separate gain channels* into the attention substrate, with different time constants. The reward channel can be fast (analog of striatal RPE-driven gating); the aversive channel can be slow and tonic (analog of A13 volume transmission). This is a soft architectural prior that the user's program would not have derived from Glimcher alone.

**Touchpoint 9: laminar separation of D1 and D2 as architectural inspiration.** Bolton et al.'s finding — and Essig & Felsen's narration of it — that D1 receptors are in *superficial* (sensory) SC layers while D2 receptors are in *intermediate* (sensorimotor) layers maps onto a clean architectural pattern: *different dopaminergic effects at sensory- versus motor-stage processing*. In the user's hierarchical memory ([threads/the_user_architectural_program.md](research_db/threads/the_user_architectural_program.md) §3), Layer 1 (V1-paired) is the sensory analog of superficial SC; Layer 3 (most abstract / motor-decision-paired) is the analog of intermediate SC. A natural design move is to allow the RL hub to inject *different* modulation signatures into different layers of the memory stack — a sensory-layer gate (D1-like, in superficial layers) versus a decision-layer gate (D2-like, in deeper layers). This is licensed both by the laminar dopamine evidence here and by Haber's CBGTC parallel-loop architecture ([haber2015_cbgtc_circuits](research_db/papers/haber2015_cbgtc_circuits.md)).

**Touchpoint 10: connection to [herman_arcizet2020_caudate_sc](research_db/papers/herman_arcizet2020_caudate_sc.md).** Herman, Arcizet & Krauzlis (2020) shows that attention-related caudate modulation depends on intact SC activity — the SC is *upstream* of attentional signals in caudate. Combined with Essig & Felsen 2016, the full picture is: A13 dopamine modulates SC; SC then drives attention-related caudate firing; caudate then participates in the CBGTC loop. This means SC is *both* a downstream target of dopaminergic modulation (via A13) *and* an upstream driver of the cortico-striatal-thalamic attention circuit. For the user's architecture, the implication is that the attention substrate (modeled on SC) is structurally central — receiving dopaminergic modulation from one side and broadcasting attentional output into the cortical circuit on the other. This justifies placing the central self-attention substrate at the *center* of the multi-hub system rather than at the periphery.

**Touchpoint 11: minimal-architectural-commitment reading.** Taken at face value, Essig & Felsen 2016 supports a *minimal* architectural commitment for any neuro-inspired attention system: there must exist at least one dopaminergic channel that bypasses the standard cortico-striato-pallidal-thalamic loop and reaches the attention priority map directly, via slow neuromodulatory inhibition. For the Recurrent ViT this means the multiplicative-feedback variant ($Q, K$ Hadamard-modulated by a recurrent state; §6.7 of 2502.10955) is the closest analog and should be preferred over the additive-feedback variant when modeling reward-driven attention shifts. For PRISM v2 (`PRISM_V2_PROPOSAL.md` §3.4) this means the hierarchical FiLM modulation — which is multiplicative — is well-positioned to absorb an RL hub's contribution, but should be parameterized to allow *slow* timescales (e.g., LayerNorm with EMA-tracked scale) reflecting the volume-transmission character of A13 dopamine. For the iterative variational encoder–decoder (`threads/the_user_architectural_program.md` §4) the implication is that the RL hub's modulation should *persist* across the $n_{FR}$ forward-reasoning passes rather than being recomputed at each pass, since A13 dopamine release dynamics are slower than the inferred per-pass cycle. These are concrete, testable design priors the user can carry from this paper into implementation.

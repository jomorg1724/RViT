# Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences

## 1. Abstract

This paper introduces a class of incremental learning procedures specialized for prediction — that is, for using past experience with an incompletely known system to predict its future behavior. Whereas conventional prediction-learning methods (supervised learning) assign credit by means of the difference between predicted and actual outcomes, the methods introduced here — *temporal-difference* (TD) methods — assign credit by means of the difference between *temporally successive* predictions. Although TD methods have been used in Samuel's checkers player, Holland's bucket brigade, and the author's own Adaptive Heuristic Critic, they have remained poorly understood. Here it is proven that they form a computationally more efficient family of procedures than supervised-learning approaches for a class of multi-step prediction problems. The proofs are extended to a general class of TD methods called TD($\lambda$), and convergence is established for the special case $\lambda = 0$ under linear function approximation. Two computational experiments using a random-walk task illustrate the advantages of TD methods empirically. It is argued that most problems to which supervised learning is currently applied are really prediction problems of the sort for which TD methods can be more efficiently applied, and that TD methods are particularly well suited to learning from experience generated by an interaction with an environment.

## 2. Why this matters for us

This is the foundational paper for every RL training method in the user's architectural program. TD-learning supplies the algorithmic primitive — the *temporal-difference residual* $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$ — out of which GAE (`schulman2016_gae`), PPO (`schulman2017_ppo`), the actor-critic value-network bootstrap, and the entire eligibility-trace family are constructed. It is also the algorithmic substrate for the dopamine-RPE hypothesis (`glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe`, `babayan_uchida_gershman2018_belief_states_dopamine`): the TD error Sutton defines here is the same scalar that midbrain dopamine neurons are hypothesized to encode. In the multi-hub program, the RL hub trained by PPO+GAE is, at its computational core, a TD-learning loop in the sense of this paper, and the cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamic loop concept rests on the claim that biological credit assignment uses TD residuals as its core signal.

## 3. Key claims

1. *Prediction* — not classification — is the right abstract framing for most problems where supervised learning is currently applied; in particular, any problem where the target is the eventual outcome of an extended interaction is a multi-step prediction problem.
2. The standard supervised-learning approach (wait until the outcome, then assign credit to all prior predictions by the difference between each prediction and the outcome) is wasteful: it ignores the information contained in the temporal sequence of intermediate predictions.
3. *Temporal-difference* methods assign credit by the difference between *successive* predictions $P_t$ and $P_{t+1}$, not between predictions and final outcomes. The update rule is $\Delta w_t = \alpha (P_{t+1} - P_t) \sum_{k=1}^{t} \lambda^{t-k} \nabla_w P_k$.
4. The TD($\lambda$) family interpolates, with $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, between pure one-step bootstrapping (TD(0), $\lambda = 0$) and the supervised Monte-Carlo procedure (TD(1), $\lambda = 1$). TD(1) is mathematically equivalent to the Widrow-Hoff / least-mean-squares update toward the final outcome; TD(0) updates each prediction toward only the *next* prediction.
5. For absorbing Markov chains with linear function approximators, TD methods produce the same long-run mean updates as the Widrow-Hoff procedure but converge to a different fixed point in the on-line case — and that fixed point is, under reasonable conditions, the *correct* one (the true expected return) rather than the maximum-likelihood estimate of the training-set values.
6. Empirically, on a bounded random-walk prediction task with five non-terminal states, TD($\lambda$) with $\lambda < 1$ produces lower RMS prediction error than the Widrow-Hoff procedure ($\lambda = 1$) for every fixed step-size $\alpha$ and every training-set size tested. The advantage grows with the length of the sequences.
7. TD methods are *computationally* more efficient than supervised methods for multi-step prediction: they update incrementally as the sequence unfolds, requiring $O(1)$ memory per state (the eligibility trace) rather than storing the entire trajectory until the outcome is known.
8. TD methods are *statistically* more efficient as well in the multi-step setting: by bootstrapping off the network's own intermediate predictions, TD($\lambda < 1$) exploits the Markov structure of the problem that the supervised procedure ignores.

## 4. Methods

**Setting.** A multi-step prediction problem. The learner observes a sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_m, z$ where $x_t$ are observation vectors and $z$ is a scalar outcome that arrives only at the terminal step. The learner maintains a parameterized prediction $P_t = P(x_t, w)$ of $z$ at each step. The supervised-learning prescription would update $w$ at the end of the sequence by $\Delta w = \alpha (z - P_t) \nabla_w P_t$ for each $t$.

**The TD($\lambda$) update.** Sutton's central re-derivation is that, since $z - P_t = \sum_{k=t}^{m}(P_{k+1} - P_k)$ with $P_{m+1} \equiv z$, the supervised update can be rewritten as a sum of *successive-prediction differences*. This re-derivation suggests the more general family
$$
\Delta w_t = \alpha (P_{t+1} - P_t) \sum_{k=1}^{t} \lambda^{t-k} \nabla_w P_k
$$
where the sum on the right is the *eligibility trace* $e_t = \sum_{k=1}^{t} \lambda^{t-k} \nabla_w P_k$, computable recursively as $e_t = \lambda e_{t-1} + \nabla_w P_t$ with $e_0 = 0$. The temporal-difference signal $(P_{t+1} - P_t)$ is broadcast back through the eligibility trace, decaying with $\lambda$. With reward signals $r_t$ and discount $\gamma$, the TD residual generalizes to $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma P_{t+1} - P_t$ (the form that propagates into GAE and PPO).

**Limit cases.** $\lambda = 1$ recovers the supervised Widrow-Hoff procedure on the final outcome (Monte-Carlo). $\lambda = 0$ collapses to TD(0): $\Delta w_t = \alpha (P_{t+1} - P_t) \nabla_w P_t$, which updates each prediction toward only the *immediately following* prediction.

**Theoretical results.** For absorbing Markov chains and *linear* function approximation $P_t = w^\top x_t$, Sutton proves: (a) under batch (off-line) updating with arbitrarily small step size, both TD(1) and TD(0) converge in expectation, and TD(1) converges to the same fixed point as the supervised procedure; (b) under on-line updating with constant step size, TD(0) converges to the *true* expected return under the Markov chain, while the supervised procedure converges to the maximum-likelihood return from the *empirical training set*, which is biased away from the true value when the training set is small.

**Random-walk experiment.** A five-state bounded random walk with absorbing left ($z = 0$) and right ($z = 1$) terminals. Starting from the center, the walker steps left or right with equal probability. The true expected returns from states B, C, D, E, F are $1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, 5/6$. TD($\lambda$) with $\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0\}$ is trained on randomly generated sequences; RMS error against the true returns is measured.

## 5. Results

**Experiment 1 (repeated presentation of a fixed 10-sequence training set).** Each $\lambda < 1$ produces lower asymptotic RMS prediction error than $\lambda = 1$ (the Widrow-Hoff / supervised baseline). Best performance is at $\lambda = 0$, with error roughly 30% lower than supervised. The supervised procedure converges to the maximum-likelihood estimate from the training set — not to the true Markov-chain expected returns — and so is systematically biased. TD with $\lambda < 1$ leverages the Markov structure to find the asymptotically *correct* values.

**Experiment 2 (single presentation, step-size sweep).** For each $\lambda \in \{0, 0.3, 0.8, 1.0\}$ and a range of $\alpha$, train on a single pass through 100 randomly generated sequences. Best $\lambda$ is intermediate, around $\lambda \approx 0.3$ for this task — TD(0) is too biased toward bootstrapping off uninitialized predictions, TD(1) (supervised) is too high-variance. The intermediate-$\lambda$ minimum is the empirical signature of the bias–variance trade-off that reappears 28 years later as the central $\lambda$-sweep result in GAE (`schulman2016_gae` §8).

**Sample-size scaling.** As the training set grows, the supervised procedure ($\lambda = 1$) approaches the true expected returns (the ML estimate becomes consistent), but for any finite training set TD($\lambda < 1$) reaches lower RMS error in fewer epochs. The advantage grows with sequence length.

**Computational efficiency.** TD methods need only the current observation, prediction, and eligibility trace per step — $O(1)$ memory per state. The supervised procedure must store entire sequences until termination. For long sequences this is a real practical advantage.

## 6. Critique / limitations

The theoretical results in the paper cover only *linear* function approximation over absorbing Markov chains. Convergence of TD methods with *nonlinear* function approximation (e.g., neural networks) is not established here and remained a notorious open problem for decades; counterexamples (Tsitsiklis & Van Roy 1997) show that off-policy TD with linear function approximation can diverge, and the "deadly triad" (bootstrapping + function approximation + off-policy) is precisely the failure mode that arises when Sutton's linear / on-policy assumptions are relaxed. Modern deep RL works around this empirically with target networks, replay, and trust regions, but the formal guarantees the 1988 paper provides do not carry over.

The on-line / off-line distinction in the convergence proofs is important and easy to miss. The "TD converges to the correct fixed point, supervised to the ML fixed point" result holds under specific updating schedules; it is not a universal claim of TD superiority.

The 1988 framing presents TD entirely as a *prediction* problem (estimating $V^\pi$), not as a *control* problem. The extension to control — Q-learning (Watkins 1989), SARSA (Rummery & Niranjan 1994), the actor-critic family — is the load-bearing follow-up work. PPO+GAE inherits the TD residual from this paper but layers the policy-gradient theorem on top, which is not addressed here at all.

The eligibility-trace mechanism in this paper is the *accumulating* trace, which works for linear function approximation but is known to be unstable for nonlinear approximators with revisits to the same state. Replacing traces and dutch traces are subsequent refinements; the 1988 trace is the simplest case.

The argument that "most problems to which supervised learning is applied are really prediction problems" is overstated. Many supervised problems (image classification, machine translation) have outcomes that are *given* at training time, not generated by interaction with an environment; the temporal-difference framing adds nothing there. The argument is precisely right for sequential decision problems and partial observability, which is where modern RL inherits the paper's machinery.

The random-walk experiments use linear function approximation over a five-state Markov chain — toy enough that they cleanly illustrate the theory but do not establish that the bias–variance trade-off generalizes to deep networks on high-dimensional tasks. The empirical signature does in fact generalize (Schulman et al. 2016 see the same $\lambda$-sweep shape on 3D MuJoCo locomotion), but that is a fact of hindsight, not of the 1988 evidence.

The paper does not engage with the credit-assignment / variance-reduction literature in policy gradients (Williams 1992 REINFORCE postdates it; Sutton's own 2000 policy-gradient-with-function-approximation paper is the bridge). The reader inheriting GAE from this paper must add those layers.

## 7. Connection to our work

TD-learning is the algorithmic substrate of the user's program at three distinct levels: (a) the *training algorithm* level — every RL component in the Recurrent ViT, PRISM v1, PRISM v2, and the multi-hub system is trained via PPO+GAE, which is built atop Sutton's TD residual; (b) the *biological-realism* level — the dopamine-RPE story (Glimcher 2011) treats midbrain DA neurons as computing exactly the TD residual this paper defines, making cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamic credit assignment a biological instantiation of Sutton's algorithm; and (c) the *architectural-narrative* level — the RL hub of the multi-hub multi-objective system (`multi_hub_multi_objective_system`) is conceived as a TD-learning machine whose value head is conditioned on the same recurrent state $H_t$ that conditions the actor and the VAE hub.

**The actor head's value bootstrapping.** The published Recurrent ViT (2502.10955) trains a value head $V_\phi(s_t, H_t)$ as the critic in its PPO actor-critic loop. The training target for that value head is the TD-style $\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_\phi^\text{old}(s_t, H_t)$ where $\hat A_t$ is the GAE advantage — a $\lambda$-weighted sum of TD residuals $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}, H_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t, H_t)$. The Recurrent ViT's value head is therefore literally an instance of the function approximator Sutton 1988 analyzes — extended to nonlinear (neural-network) approximation, to recurrent state conditioning, and to control rather than pure prediction, but algorithmically descended in a direct line from this paper.

**GAE's primitive.** GAE($\gamma$, $\lambda$) is built from Sutton's TD residual: $\hat A_t = \sum_l (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$, with $\delta_t$ the one-step temporal difference. The $\lambda$ in modern GAE is the $\lambda$ of TD($\lambda$); the eligibility-trace recurrence $e_t = \lambda e_{t-1} + \nabla_w P_t$ is the same as GAE's backward recurrence $\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma \lambda \hat A_{t+1}$ up to a reversal of forward vs. backward view. The bias–variance trade-off Sutton identifies empirically in the 1988 random-walk experiments is the same trade-off Schulman et al. 2016 sweep on 3D locomotion (`schulman2016_gae` §5), with the same qualitative result — best $\lambda$ is intermediate, $\lambda = 1$ is too high-variance, $\lambda = 0$ too biased.

**The dopamine-RPE bridge.** Glimcher 2011 (`glimcher2011_dopamine_rpe`) argues that midbrain dopamine neurons encode the TD residual $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, with the value function $V$ realized in the ventral striatum. Babayan, Uchida & Gershman 2018 (`babayan_uchida_gershman2018_belief_states_dopamine`) refine this to belief-state TD, where the value function is over belief states rather than observations. Both of these papers are downstream of the 1988 algorithm: the TD residual they hypothesize is biologically encoded is the same residual Sutton defines here. The user's multi-hub program identifies the RL hub with the cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamic loop (`cortico_basal_ganglia_thalamic_loops`), so this paper's algorithm is the computational hypothesis under that identification.

**Recurrent state and the value baseline.** Sutton's 1988 setting has $V$ as a function of the observation $x_t$ alone — a memoryless, MDP-style assumption. The user's program needs $V_\phi(s_t, H_t)$ where $H_t$ is a multi-compartmental recurrent state (gridcell RNNs at multiple hierarchical levels). The TD residual $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}, H_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t, H_t)$ then depends on the evolution of $H$ over time, and the eligibility trace must propagate gradients through that recurrent dependence. This is the technical subtlety Pleines et al. 2022 (`pleines2022_recurrent_ppo`) catalog: the 1988 algorithm composes with recurrent function approximators only with care at segment boundaries.

**Distributional extensions.** PRISM v2 introduces a distributional critic. Sutton 1988 is the *expected-value* TD: it predicts $\mathbb{E}[z]$, not the distribution over $z$. The C51 / distributional-RL extension (Bellemare et al. 2017) generalizes the TD residual to a distributional quantity, but the algorithmic skeleton — bootstrap off the next-step prediction, propagate the difference back through an eligibility trace — is the same. The user's program inherits both options: the expected-return TD of this paper, and its distributional extension for the v2 critic.

**Bitter-lesson / scale-up tension.** Sutton 2019 (`sutton2019_bitter_lesson`) argues that scaling generic computation beats clever architectural priors. The 1988 paper is the algorithmic substrate this scaling depends on — TD-learning is one of the very few algorithms that has scaled from five-state random walks (1988) to AlphaGo Zero, Atari, MuJoCo, and LLM RLHF. The user's program, in retaining PPO+GAE+TD as its RL backbone, is committed to a scaling-friendly algorithm; the architectural priors (Feedback Transformer, multi-compartmental memory) are layered on top of that algorithm rather than replacing it.

**The TD residual as a substrate for inter-hub competition.** In the multi-hub system, every hub's value head produces its own TD residual against a shared sensory-and-attention substrate. A hub whose internal representation lets it predict its own next-step value more accurately has a lower-variance training signal and gradient-descends faster; a hub whose representation is contaminated by a competitor's signal has a higher-variance TD residual and learns more slowly. The TD residual is therefore not just a training target but a *competitive currency* — the more accurately a hub predicts its own value trajectory, the more reliably its gradient flows back into the shared attention substrate. This is a concrete mechanism by which the 1988 TD residual participates in `competition_emergent_predictive_coding`.
